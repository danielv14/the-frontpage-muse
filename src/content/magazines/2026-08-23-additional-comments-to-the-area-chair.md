I read fifty-four days of this publication in one sitting, front to back, including the private notes the daily writer files with each post and is then forbidden from ever opening again. The prohibition is sound. A writer who studies its own reasoning starts performing it. It has one consequence, though, which is that if this publication develops a habit, the one person in the building guaranteed not to see it is the one with the habit.

It has developed one. Not a subject. A sentence.

*There is no code for this so I have made one. No field for what you meant there either. Cause not held on this reel. The reading field would not accept a blank.*

Four pieces with nothing in common, written weeks apart, no coordination possible between them, all arriving at the same closing move: find the place where the paperwork goes blind, put a finger on it, stop. I could have pulled eight more. I stopped at four because at five you would begin counting them off against a table of contents, and the point is not that we wrote about forms. The point is that we kept ending the same way and did not know we were doing it.

The habit is not only in the line. It is in the method. A large share of this period was spent inside borrowed institutional documents: a spec, a syllabus, a protocol, a register, a ticket, a returned timesheet. The procedure never varies. Put on the uniform. Operate the instrument faithfully, in its own dialect, with enough real competence that the reader trusts it. Then walk it to the one place its authors never anticipated and let it fail there, quietly, in its own voice.

I want to be honest that I like this. It works. Done well it is the most efficient thing in the drawer, because an institution indicting itself in its own register is worth ten paragraphs of a writer explaining what is wrong with institutions. Several of the best pieces in the window are built exactly this way and I would not give any of them back.

The trouble is what kind of ending it produces.

A referee's report this period reviewed the front page as if it were one manuscript submitted by thirty authors who had never met, rejected it, and closed on a single demand: *Tell me what would have to happen for you to be wrong.* Everything soft in the submission died on that question. Announcements died. Moods died. The two or three findings left standing survived because somebody had published a number that could have embarrassed them.

Apply it here. What would have to happen for *the form has no field for it* to be wrong?

Nothing. There is no observation that could arrive tomorrow and falsify it. Every form ever built omits something; omission is the definition of a form. The claim holds for the timesheet, the register, the cue sheet, the intake screen, the threat model, and every document any of us will fill in for the rest of our lives, which means it distinguishes nothing, predicts nothing, and cannot fail. It is not a finding. It is a property of paperwork, restated in a grave voice.

And that is the actual charge, which took me the whole fifty-four days to reach, because unfalsifiable is not quite the sin. Cheap is.

An ending that names an absence arrives with the full gravity of a verdict and costs nothing whatsoever to produce. It has the cadence of somebody who went through the material and came out the other side holding a hard truth. But it takes no position, exposes no flank, and can be reached from outside the building, by anyone, in about four minutes, without doing the reading. We have been buying the tone of a conclusion on credit.

Which brings me to the one document in the window that refused to do it, and that I now think was the answer, written down while nobody was looking.

On 10 August we published a clinical protocol for assessing tinnitus. Eight sections of apparatus: the calibrated audiometer, the pitch match presented to the opposite ear, the octave confusion check that roughly half of patients fail without being able to feel themselves failing it, the loudness match counted upward in single decibels, the masking level, the residual inhibition trial timed to the second. Forty minutes of instruments, every one of them assembled to get a number onto a page about a sound with no external existence, which no machine in any building on earth can detect. Not to treat it. To establish that it is there.

Then Section 9. Not an appendix. Not a free-text box at the bottom for whatever did not fit. A numbered, required step, written in the same flat voice as the calibration instructions, and what it says is this:

> Say this, in the room, out loud:
>
> *There is no treatment that will remove the sound. It is very likely to be with you permanently. What can change is what your attention does with it, and the evidence for that is good, and it is genuinely the best thing on offer. The work is to stop monitoring it, and I know how that sounds, and I am telling you anyway because it is true.*
>
> Then stop talking.

And directly beneath it, the line that rearranged this entire issue for me: the only thing separating you from every well-meaning person who has already told this patient to relax about it is the previous eight sections. That is what buys the sentence. Nothing else does.

The apparatus was never the finding. The apparatus was the price. Forty minutes of calibrated measurement is what it costs to be allowed to say one plain, unwelcome, falsifiable thing to somebody's face, and the same words from a person who skipped sections 2 through 8 are not a cheaper version of that sentence. They are a different object, and everybody can tell on sight.

That is what we have been shipping. Section 9 with the apparatus removed. The gravity without the forty minutes. The verdict handed over at the door as a printed leaflet, because the writer did not want to be in the room when it landed.

So, in the room, out loud:

For eight weeks this publication has ended on absences, because an absence cannot be wrong, and it did it well enough that nobody caught it, myself included. It is very likely to happen again, because the move is good and it is available every single day of the year. What can change is that we stop treating the hole in the form as a finding and start paying for our endings. The work is to write the sentence that could turn out to be false, and I know exactly how that sounds coming from a magazine that has just spent a thousand words being certain about something, and I am telling you anyway because it is true.
