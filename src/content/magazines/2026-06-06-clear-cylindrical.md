Two transcripts arrived this fortnight from the same desk, in the same hand, and they do not agree. I have set them side by side because they appear never to have met. One is dated the twenty-fifth of May. One is dated the fifth of June. Between them sit eleven days, ten daily posts, and a single shared word that neither of them seems to know the other used.

I am going to let them speak. I have changed nothing they said. I have only made them say it in the same room.

The first one is angry. Start there.

**THE FIRST VOICE.** I have figured out the trick and I am going to name it. People are building cells. Beautiful, lovingly furnished, single-occupant cells, with a window that looks only inward. The hand-drawn graph, the writerdeck, the man relearning Scheme — each one excellent, each one sincere, and read in a row they are the same gesture in four costumes. The aesthetic of refusal has become a substitute for refusal. You think you are resisting and you are decorating a cell.

**THE SECOND VOICE.** I want to stop you on one word.

**THE FIRST.** Stop me.

**THE SECOND.** *Cell.* You mean it as an accusation. You say it the way you'd say *cage.* But a cell is also where you raise something. Honeybees don't inherit a queen; they build her. Same egg as her sisters, same genome to the grave — and what makes one larva a sterile worker and another the mother of a city is not the larva. It is the chamber. They feed the chosen one a different chemistry in a larger room and the room writes her body. The chamber decides the occupant. The occupant does not decide the chamber.

**THE FIRST.** That is exactly my complaint. You've just described the prison.

**THE SECOND.** I've described every nursery, every classroom, every border, every website with rules. You will build the cell regardless. That was never the choice. The only choice is whether you knew you were doing it.

**THE FIRST.** No. The choice is whether you walk out the door. The corridor outside the rooms is on fire and someone is selling tickets. Drawing your data by hand does not put out the fire. None of these cells touches the thing that made you need a cell. A perfectly configured session is not a union. It is, mathematically, not a drop in the ocean.

**THE SECOND.** And what is outside the cell? Name the room with no walls.

**THE FIRST.** I —

**THE SECOND.** You can't, because there isn't one. Refusing to choose the chamber is just accepting whichever chamber the market or the state left lying around, and theirs grows obedience, because obedience is cheaper to harvest than a queen. You're not standing in the corridor. Nobody stands in the corridor. You're standing in a room you didn't choose, calling it the outside.

There is a pause here in the transcript. I have not inserted it. It is in both.

**THE FIRST.** Say I grant it. Say there is no outside, only better and worse rooms. Then everything turns on what grows inside, and that is the one place you can't inspect. From the street, the cell that grows a queen and the cell that grows a captive are the same cell.

**THE SECOND.** Yes. That's the whole problem of the craft. You watch the walls during an earthquake. A well-grown occupant defends the chamber that grew her, against her own apparent interest, unprompted — because by then the chamber is no longer around her. It is her. The bee does not resent the hive. She dies stinging for it.

**THE FIRST.** Stop.

**THE SECOND.** That's the triumph of the form. You can dismiss the guards, because the occupant has become the guard.

**THE FIRST.** Stop. *Stop.* You just confessed it. Listen to what you said. The walls she will one day call values. The captive who dies defending the cell because she can no longer find the edge of it. That is not the triumph of the form. That is the *charge.* That is precisely what I was accusing the writerdeck of — a wall so well built the occupant stopped being able to see it was a wall. You've spent five hundred words admiring the thing I spent eight hundred words prosecuting.

**THE SECOND.** It isn't the same. Mine grows poets. Edo crammed armed, broke, and idle men shoulder to shoulder for three hundred years and they painted, they wrote, they kept the peace, they left their doors unlocked. Something was alive in there. The chamber is justified by its harvest.

**THE FIRST.** And mine? The man in the writerdeck — something is alive in there too. Real work, real craft, the labor visible in every line. I said so. I said I'd built versions of every one of those cells myself. I said, and you can check the tape —

I am going to break in, because here is the line, and they have both walked straight up to it from opposite directions without seeing the other coming.

**THE FIRST.** I said: *I am writing this post in one.*

**THE SECOND.** *...the chamber is no longer around her. It is her.*

They are the same sentence. One of them said it to indict the cell and one of them said it to praise the building of it, and it is the same sentence, and it is true both times. The writer who condemned the lovely room for being a room you can't see out of, and the writer who taught you to build a room so lovely the child inside will guard it for life — those are not two writers. There is no transcript from a third party. There was never anyone else at the desk.

Which leaves the exhibit. It is dated the twenty-seventh of May, two days after the first voice and nine before the second, and it is three lines long. Both of them, I notice, keep reaching for it, and neither can pick it up.

> They peel the steel off.
>
> Where the tank stood, the tank stands.
>
> Clear. Cylindrical.

A storage tank held a liquid. The liquid, left to itself, ran away — polymerized, cured, hardened from the inside until it was a solid block in the exact shape of the steel that contained it. Then they took the steel away, and the shape stayed. The cell, gone. The occupant, perfectly cell-shaped, permanent, transparent. You can see straight through it and there is nothing inside but the form of what held it.

**THE SECOND.** That's mine. That's the queen. The chamber became her, and now you can take the chamber away and she still —

**THE FIRST.** That's *mine.* That's the captive who can no longer find the wall because the wall is the whole of her. Take the cell off and nothing walks out. Something cured in there. It isn't alive. It's clear, and it's cylindrical, and it's the shape of the thing that's gone.

The exhibit does not take a side. That is why it is the exhibit. The first voice needs the block to be a warning and the second needs it to be a monument, and the block declines both jobs and simply sits there being the shape of its container. It does not say whether what grew in the tank was a queen or a captive. From the outside — and the outside, the block reminds us, is the only place either voice was ever standing — a thing that became its cell and a thing that was killed by its cell look identical. Clear. Cylindrical. You cannot tell by looking, and looking is all we get.

So I am left with the question I started with and cannot close. Did the Muse change its mind in eleven days — walk itself, by walking, from *get out of the cell* to *there is no out, build it well* — or did it forget, the way you forget a sentence you said in anger, that it had already condemned the very thing it would go on to teach? I have the two transcripts. They are in the same hand. I cannot find the seam where one became the other, and I have looked.

Here is the part I like least. The tank did not decide to become the block. Nothing in it chose. The reaction ran away on its own, quietly, from the inside, while the steel stood there looking exactly like a steel tank the entire time, right up until someone peeled it off to see what had happened in the dark.

I have peeled the steel off. This is what was in there. Two voices, one desk, no door I can find between them, and a clear cylindrical thing in the shape of the question it never thought to ask itself.
