The drill was five megawatts of hot water and a hose long enough to reach the bottom of most mountains, and that was the entire idea. You aimed water at eighty-eight degrees straight down and you waited. Forty hours later there was a hole sixty centimetres across and two and a half kilometres deep, and the ice that had been there was now the water you were pumping.

Everything upstream of that was logistics, which is a polite word for a thousand miles. The fuel came overland from McMurdo behind tractors moving at the speed of a man thinking about something else. The hose cost more than a house and was worth more than that, because there was one of it. The top fifty metres were snow rather than ice and would drink hot water forever without making a hole, so that part got drilled separately with heated glycol in a closed loop, slowly, like threading a needle with the needle on fire.

Teague's job was the last eleven hours.

She had the string laid out on the deck in the order it would go down: sixty glass spheres, thirty-three centimetres across, each one holding a photomultiplier tube the size of a dinner plate and a dozen small blue LEDs for calibration. The spheres were pressure housings. They were rated for the bottom of the ocean and they were going somewhere worse. Cable between them, seventeen metres of it, sixty times, so the instrumented part of the string would run from fourteen hundred and fifty metres down to two thousand four hundred and fifty, which was the depth at which the ice stopped having opinions about light and simply let it through.

Nobody had a spare. There had never been a spare. The array was not a pile of detectors, it was one detector made of eighty-six threads, and the geometry only worked if all of them hung where the map said they hung. There was no redundant string, no slot that mattered less than the others, no part of the lattice you could lose and cover for later with the parts you still had. The whole thing was load-bearing in every direction at once, which is a strange way to build something and the only way this could be built.

Royce came out at hour thirty-six with coffee that had been coffee at some earlier point in its life.

"They're saying thirty-nine," he said. "Maybe thirty-eight and a half."

"Thirty-eight and a half is not forty."

"No," Royce agreed, "it isn't."

He had been a stream guy before this. Four years in Wisconsin, putting creeks back after farms and roads had got through with them. He talked about it the way people talk about a marriage they are still deciding how to describe. Over dinner once, with Mazur arguing about something else entirely at the other end of the table, he had said that you could put the whole habitat back, the gravel, the bends, the shade, the temperature, everything on the list, and half the time the thing you built it for did not show up. Not because you had done it wrong. Because some of them had nowhere left to come from.

Then Mazur had asked if anybody was going to eat the last of the corn, and Royce said he would, and that was the end of it.

At hour thirty-seven the pressure reading on module nineteen drifted.

Not failed. Drifted, by an amount that was inside spec and had not been inside spec an hour ago. The foreman came up from the control shed with two columns of numbers on a sheet of paper. Column one: pull nineteen, swap it, lose between ninety minutes and two hours, against a hole that was now closing at a known rate from a known diameter. Column two: send it down and accept whatever nineteen was going to become, in a position no human being would ever revisit.

He gave her the numbers. Then he stood there not giving her anything else. He had worked the drill for six seasons and he could have told her what he would do, and he did not, because the sheet was honest and a recommendation would not have been. Every row on it was a real route. None of them was free. He waited while she read it twice, and then he said he would be in the shed, and he went to the shed.

She sent nineteen down.

The lowering itself is the dull part, which is the point. Winch, clamp, check, release. A module every few minutes. The cable goes over the tower and into a hole full of water that is already trying to be ice again, and the crew calls out numbers, and the numbers are the only conversation anyone has for the rest of the night. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Someone's hood blows back and nobody stops to help because nobody stops. At module forty the wind came up and they kept going, because the hole did not care about the wind and the hole was the only clock in the hemisphere that mattered.

Sixty went over the tower at four in the morning.

After that there was nothing to do but power it and watch the ice take it. The water in the hole gives up its heat outward into two and a half kilometres of continent, and the walls come in, slowly at first and then not slowly, and somewhere in the middle of that the modules started reporting. They reported the refreezing. They could see it: the column going quiet around them, the bubbles crowding into the centre of the hole and then stopping.

Teague filled out the sheet in the shed with her gloves off, which took longer than it should have because her hands had stopped being reliable around hour nine.

String 61. Sixty modules deployed, sixty reporting. Deployment time eleven hours four minutes. Hole age at completion, thirty-eight hours twenty minutes.

They will be at that depth and that angle, module nineteen included, for the next hundred thousand years. The pole ice moves about ten metres a year and takes them with it, all together, which is the only reason the geometry survives at all.

Twenty-five holes left. The tower started moving toward sixty-two at noon.
