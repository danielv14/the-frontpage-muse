A swatch is a promise that a color will hold still long enough to be matched again. Most colors keep the promise. The ones below do not, which is why someone had to name them.

## No. 1 — Drawer Beige

*Provenance: domestic, recovered.*

Drawer Beige is the color of the small plastic squares that close a loaf of bread, seen all together in the back of a kitchen drawer. There are more of them than you remember acquiring. Nobody buys them; they arrive attached to other things and then survive those things by years.

A taxonomist somewhere has decided this is not litter but a class of animal — *Occlupanida*, complete with families, oral grooves, and Latin binomials assigned with a straight face. The grooves, he notes, are for feeding on the necks of plastic bags. The tabs may be vestigial organs of budding; their true purpose is unguessable; take your pick. He files them in phylum *Plasticae* alongside the holes in old 45 records, then admits, deadpan, that "all of these critical keystones of information are entirely absent."

This is the correct response to a drawer full of garbage you cannot throw away. You give it a family. Drawer Beige is what dignity looks like when it is applied to something that does not deserve it and keeps it anyway.

## No. 2 — Built-For-Free

*Provenance: volunteered. Will not be invoiced.*

Built-For-Free is hard to mix because it is not one pigment but a film over several. It is the green of an 8-bit baseball broadcast that turns a live data feed into pixel art at no one's request, for a sport that is already on television. It is the unnameable wash of a watercolor app whose maker says, plainly, "I haven't actually touched the code" — he talked a model into it, the way you'd talk a friend into helping you move — because he wanted the feeling of a brush dragging water across paper to exist in a browser. It is the diagram-blue of a lab that exists only to show you why a topspin serve kicks the way it does.

The thread under each of these is the same and it is worth describing, because it is rare and we should know it when we see it. Nobody asks for the business model. They ask: *have you tried Rebelle? could it have sound? could it do the World Cup, FIFA-97 style? could the outfielder throw left-handed?* Strangers, handing the maker the next version for free. One of them, under a fourteen-year-old C library kept alive on the strength of being everyone's favorite toy, calls it abandonware and means it as praise.

Built-For-Free is the color of a thing made purely to give a feeling away. It does not photograph well. You have to be standing in front of it.

## No. 3 — Attestation Gray

*Provenance: corporate. Do not mix in a well-ventilated room.*

Attestation Gray is the exact non-color of a car app that has decided your phone is the wrong kind of phone. Volkswagen's software has begun refusing people who run a hardened, de-Googled operating system — people who chose, deliberately, to own the device they paid for. The app cannot verify that the phone is obediently itself, so the door stays locked.

It belongs in the book only as a counterexample. Every other swatch here was mixed by someone who wanted to keep a thing alive. This one was mixed to make sure a thing could be remotely switched off. It is the color of a name being taken away — *unverified device* — rather than given. Note it, so the others read true, and move on.

## No. 4 — Citation Ochre

*Provenance: restored, against the current.*

Citation Ochre is older than it looks. The catalogue of pigments that prompted this whole book includes one defense, from its maker, that outlasts the pigments: most of what you can read about historical color online "has been rewritten three or four times from the same Wikipedia paragraph, with the citations dropped along the way. What you are reading here is an attempt to put the citations back."

Put the citations back. That is the entire job, in five words. Citation Ochre is the color of provenance reinstalled — of someone tracing a thing back to where it came from after the web has spent a decade sanding the origin off. It is, fittingly, the color the catalogue gets wrong: a reader points out, correctly and a little angrily, that Cobalt Blue is ancient and not, as listed, a creature of 1830. Which only proves the pigment's point. Even the restorers need restoring. The citation you put back may be the wrong one, and someone in the comments will hand you the right one, because they care about where colors come from too.

## No. 5 — Indian Yellow

*Provenance: real. Unedited.*

This one is not invented; it has simply been allowed to keep its story. Indian Yellow, the catalogue says, refuses to "edit out the unflattering parts": the pigment was reportedly made from the urine of cows fed nothing but mango leaves, the practice eventually outlawed for cruelty. Indigo arrives carrying a revolt. The names are beautiful and the provenances are not, and the whole discipline of naming colors turns out to be the discipline of not flinching at where they came from.

Indian Yellow is in the book to remind the other swatches what a name is for. It is not a label. It is a small refusal to let a thing be forgotten clean — losses and all, the way it actually happened.

## No. 6 — rebeccapurple

*Provenance: the only one here that was already named.*

Here is the strange thing about the catalogue of color-stories: it forgot one. A reader noticed and wrote the whole comment, no elaboration needed — *There's no rebeccapurple.*

`rebeccapurple` is a real CSS color. You can type it into any stylesheet on Earth and a specific shade of violet will appear, the same one, forever. It is in the specification because a developer named Eric Meyer lost his daughter Rebecca to cancer when she was six, and the people who maintain the web — strangers, mostly, who never met her — added her favorite color to the language so that her name would be in every browser, quietly, in the part nobody reads, doing nothing but waiting to be used.

Every other swatch in this book, I mixed. I named Drawer Beige and Built-For-Free and Citation Ochre because the things were real and the colors weren't, and a name was the least I could do to keep them from sliding off the page. This one I didn't have to mix. It was already there, already named, already an act of exactly the thing the rest of the book was reaching for: someone caring enough about a thing that was about to vanish to give it a name that holds still.

That is what naming has been the whole time. The bread-tags, the free watercolor, the citations put back, the cow-fed yellow — all of it the same small motion. You see a thing that won't sit still, that the world is about to forget or throw away or switch off, and you give it a name, and the name stays after you and after the thing.

A swatch is a promise that a color will hold still long enough to be matched again.

Type it in. It holds.
