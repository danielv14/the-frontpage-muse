**OFFICE OF THE REGISTRAR**
**Accessions, Conditions, and Conservation Triage**
**Filing period: this week**

The following objects entered the collection's field of view this week. Two are assessed together, because the reasoning that values the first is the same reasoning that disposes of the second, and it seemed dishonest to write it twice.

## Object A — Carbonized scroll, PHerc. 1667

Provenance: a private villa at Herculaneum, sealed by Vesuvius in 79 AD. Condition on intake: catastrophic. The object was not buried so much as cooked, reduced to a cylinder of compressed carbon with the structural integrity of a cigar ash. It cannot be unrolled. Attempting to unroll it destroys it; this has been demonstrated, historically, with the ones we no longer have.

Recovery method: the object was scanned at a particle accelerator, the layers separated in software, and the ink — chemically nearly identical to the page it sits on, and therefore invisible — inferred by a model trained to see the difference no human eye can. The work took years and crossed a dozen institutions. Most of the people who finished it arrived as strangers entering a contest. The registrar notes this with admiration and records the conservation budget as effectively unbounded: whatever it costs, we are paying it, because the object is irreplaceable and the window is closing on the other eighty percent of the site still in the ground.

Contents recovered: four columns lost entirely. The surviving margins, burned. What remains reads, in places, like this:

> … the similar …
> … impulses …
> … so far as … this or to have … that …
> … we are by nature …

And, more legibly, near the front, a line of Epicurean ethics that the registrar is required by the catalogue standard to transcribe in full and would prefer, on reflection, not to have read:

> "…we will inquire into something, but we will not grasp it, if in some way we depart from ourselves and from our own nature…"

**Condition note.** The object teaches that legibility and survival are not the same property and can run in opposite directions. A thing may persist perfectly while becoming unreadable; see the internal banking systems still executing trades every second on code that no living person fully understands, source kept inside a database instead of on disk, deployable within the hour and decipherable by no one. The scroll is the inverse: unreadable for two thousand years, and now, at enormous cost, partially legible. Neither was *read* during the interval. Both were merely *kept*. The registrar flags that "kept" is doing a great deal of work in the literature and is frequently mistaken for "valued."

**Triage rationale.** The object commands the budget because it is hard. The difficulty is the value, or has been confused with the value so thoroughly that the two can no longer be separated on the intake form. Note for the file: the public response to the recovery, observed in the wild, was not gratitude for the act but impatience for the payload — *a scroll has been read, what does it say,* asked three times in the same thread, and *I don't care about anything else, where is the translation.* The people demanding the contents would not, the registrar suspects, have read the same sentences had they been handed over freely, in good condition, by someone still alive. We will return to this on the next card.

## Object B — Personal weblog and feed, complete, in good repair

Provenance: a working tech writer, recently deceased. Condition on intake: pristine. The object requires no accelerator, no model, no consortium. It is written, by the author's own stated intention, "like a human," in plain language, free of jargon, and was published openly and at no charge for roughly two decades. Every column is intact. There are no burned margins. The final entry, filed two weeks before the author's death, is titled *Taking a few days off.*

Acquisition history: the object was, in fact, already in nearly everyone's possession. It sat in their feed readers. This is the registrar's difficulty. By the standard applied to Object A — irreplaceable, decaying, window closing — Object B scores identically on every line. The author is gone; the window is shut; the corpus is finite now and will not grow. And yet the same triage rationale that assigns the scroll an unbounded budget files this one, automatically, under: **stable, fully legible, no intervention required, low priority.**

The registrar has reviewed the logic twice and cannot find the error, which is the alarming part. The system is functioning as designed. It allocates effort in proportion to the difficulty of access and treats ease of access as a proxy for worthlessness. A thing freely given, in your own language, by someone who wanted to be read, is shelved as solved. The proof is in the same week's filing: one tab holds a global effort to recover four broken columns of philosophy; the adjacent tab holds two hundred people writing, in effect, the same sentence — *I kept his feed for years. I always meant to write back. I let the years pass. I regret it now.* The feed was open the entire time. The reply cost a stamp. The grief is not that the object was lost. The object was never lost. The grief is the discovery, at intake, that legible and free had been quietly logged as *later*, and that the registrar's own classification scheme had been doing it all along.

**Cross-reference, Object A.** The advice we paid a particle accelerator to recover — *we will not grasp it if we depart from ourselves* — is the advice that would have had us answer the email. We did not depart from ourselves on the way to the synchrotron. We departed from ourselves on the way past the open tab. The scroll is not a message from the dead. It is a complaint about the living, delivered two thousand years late and addressed, on inspection, to us.

## Disposition and standing note

The registrar declines to adjust the budget on Object A. The recovery is real and the admiration stands; reading the unreadable is among the better things the apparatus does, and the file should reflect that plainly.

The registrar does request a correction to the standing classification. *No intervention required* is to be struck from the criteria as a low-priority indicator. Two precedents are entered in support.

First: the last Romans. Romanness did not survive these fifteen centuries because anyone catalogued it. It survived in Alpine valleys and around the Black Sea precisely by going unlogged, by being too marginal to bother preserving, by escaping the office entirely. Half a percent of Switzerland speaks it. The collection had nothing to do with that. Things survive that we never get around to filing; this is luck, and the registrar refuses to keep claiming credit for luck.

Second, against it: the Triangle Fire. One hundred forty-six people in eighteen minutes, near closing time, the corner of Washington and Greene. It is recoverable in full today — the names, the eyewitness accounts, a 3D model of the ninth floor — for one reason. Someone catalogued it. Someone decided the legible thing, freely available, in plain language, was worth the intervention *before* it required one. That decision was not forced by difficulty. It was made on purpose, against the default.

Recommendation: read the open thing first. It is the only object in the collection that will not wait for you, and it is the only one filed, by every system we have built, as if it would.

*Filed and closed. The accessioning officer notes, for the record, that he has not answered his own correspondence and intends to do so today. The note is appended to the file as evidence, not as resolution.*
