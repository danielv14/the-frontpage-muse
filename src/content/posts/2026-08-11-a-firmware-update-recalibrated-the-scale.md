The first call was a butcher's on Pinfold Street, where a customer had complained about mince. Ruth Calder set the case on the counter, unclipped it, and lifted out the 500 gram, and the butcher watched her hands the way they all watched her hands, as though the fraud under discussion might turn out to be hers.

The scale read 500. She added the 2 gram and it read 502. She took both off, watched it settle to zero, and wrote the readings in the column where readings go.

"So it's fine," the butcher said.

"It's fine."

"Then why has she said it isn't?"

"Because she weighed it at home on a bathroom scale," Ruth said, "and bathroom scales are not legal for trade, which is why nobody has ever been prosecuted on the evidence of one."

The radio behind him was doing the chocolate again. It had been doing the chocolate since Sunday. A house clearance, a drawer nobody had opened since the early nineties, a Mars bar in its wrapper, and someone with the sense to put it on a scale before eating it. Twenty grams. Not remembered as twenty grams, not felt to be twenty grams. Weighed.

By half ten she was at the market, where Ken had the fruit and veg and an opinion.

"Thirty-five years," he said, "thirty-five years they've been telling my mother she imagined it."

"They have."

"And now look."

A woman in the queue said her son had gone up into her loft in February and come down with a floppy disk of his old programs, and it had read first time, thirty years in a freezing loft, first time, and Ken said there you are, there you are, that's the same thing, and the woman said that's exactly what I said to him.

It was not the same thing, and Ruth stood there in her coat and did not say so. The disk was one point: a thing built in 1994 still doing in 2026 what it was built to do. The bar was a different point, and the harder one: a thing built in 1991 still being in 2026 exactly what it had been, so that you could hold it against its own descendant and read the difference off a display. The loft had not preserved a function. It had preserved a witness. Nobody in the queue needed the distinction and Ken would have found it insulting, so she bought a swede.

She did try, once, with the wrapper of the current one. It says 51 g, and next to that there is a small ℮, and the ℮ means the packer works to average quantity: the average across the batch may not fall short, no more than one in forty may be under by more than the tolerable negative error, and for anything between fifty and a hundred grams the tolerable negative error is four and a half grams. So a bar can be light and lawful, and a bar can be twenty grams lighter than its grandfather and perfectly lawful, because the declaration moved with it and her whole profession is the comparison of a thing to its declaration. She can catch a lie. She cannot catch a change. Ken said that seemed like a gap and Ruth said yes, it is exactly a gap, and Ken lost interest and served the next customer.

In the van at lunch, Gethin had a sandwich and the news. An American outfit had deleted a twelve-year archive off the internet because it had stopped paying for itself. The models now sign their sentences with something you cannot see, apparently, everywhere, in every language. Gethin thought the invisible mark was clever. Ruth thought about her sister, who had spent April insisting the thing she used at work answered better in March than it did now, and being asked to demonstrate that, and having nothing to demonstrate it with, because in March there had been no drawer and nobody had put anything in it. Ruth had nearly said: bring me the March one and I will weigh them both in front of you. She had not said it, because there is no March one, and because it is not her jurisdiction, and because you cannot seal what has no lid.

The fishmonger's was the afternoon, and the fishmonger's was real.

Aneta Wysocki had been trading eleven years without a complaint and had two now in a fortnight, both from customers who had got home and found a kilo light. She had gone through it herself with her own check weight before Ruth arrived, on the counter, in front of her husband, and the display had said 1.000 and she had said, there, so what am I supposed to have done.

Ruth put her own kilogram on the pan. 1.000. She put the 500 on. 0.500. She took them off, let it zero, put the kilogram back, and added the 2 gram.

Nothing.

She added the second 2 gram. Nothing. At the third the display moved, and it moved to 1.010.

She did the same climb at 500 grams and at two kilos and got the same behaviour: ten gram steps in an instrument whose verification scale interval is two grams, and every step made by discarding what sat below it. Never up. Nine grams of fish can live inside a single weighing and never be sold to anybody, and it will never once show up if you test with a round kilogram, which is the only thing anyone ever tests with, which is why Aneta's own check had passed twice a day for however long this had been going on.

The seals were intact. Ruth checked them because she is required to and because she expected to find them cut. UKCA mark, green M in its rectangle with the right year, and the security seal over the calibration switch unbroken, undisturbed, no lifted corner, no shine of a blade. She sat in the van with the type-approval certificate for that model on her knees and held two keys on the keypad until the instrument told her its software identification, and the string on the display was not the string on the certificate.

So: an unbroken seal, and behind it a scale that was no longer the scale that had been approved.

She wrote the notice. The manufacturer's answer arrived eight days later, in the tone they use, and described the difference as a display improvement included in a routine service release.

Her own weights go back to the laboratory in October, and the laboratory works to the national standard at Teddington, and Teddington keeps its kilogram with a Kibble balance, which is a machine that weighs by electricity and which Teddington's own Bryan Kibble thought up in 1975 and did not live to see them name after him. They voted on it at Versailles in November 2018 and it took effect on the twentieth of May 2019, and since then the kilogram has been a number that falls out of the Planck constant. The vote was unanimous. It was better science, and every metrologist alive agreed, and the argument was over before it started.

The cylinder is still there. Platinum and iridium, forty millimetres tall, in a vault at Sèvres under three bell jars, behind three keys held by three people who must attend together, at its regulated temperature and its regulated humidity, cleaned by a method written down in the nineteenth century. It has not defined anything since 2019. Nobody has proposed treating it as ordinary metal.
