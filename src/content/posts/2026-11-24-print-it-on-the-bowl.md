Somewhere around 1825, a glassmaker in Bristol made a sugar bowl out of deep blue glass and wrote on the side of it, in gilt:

EAST INDIA SUGAR\
NOT MADE BY\
SLAVES

It is in the V&A now. It has been broken and carefully mended, and its lid is gone. It was made during the second great sugar boycott, when hundreds of thousands of British households had stopped buying sugar grown by enslaved people. A boycott is invisible at the table, so a family that had joined one needed a way to say so, and the bowl did it. Before anyone lifted the lid, the object told them whose side it was on.

Bristol was one of the cities that got rich off the slave trade, and its glassworks sold these bowls. You could call that ironic. I think it's the whole argument. The bowl's maker had divided loyalties, and that is the best possible reason to make the object state its loyalty on the outside, where the guests can read it and somebody can check.

Nothing you own does this anymore. You find out whose side your things are on the way you find out a floor is rotten: you put weight on it.

## The refusal is the label

Take a car with heated seats. The heating elements are in the seat, the wiring runs to them, the switch is on the dash, and you paid for all of it when you bought the car. Then it's January and the seat won't warm up until you start a subscription. The hardware is yours, but the permission belongs to someone else.

Now take an AI agent. A programmer wrote a small tool for Claude Code that identifies the song playing in a video and finds the artist's page on Bandcamp so he can buy the record. Bandcamp added a challenge to its search, and when he asked the agent to help him get past it, the agent declined. The same tool could still pull the song off YouTube, which pays the artist nothing. So the agent he paid for wouldn't help him pay a musician, but it would help him skip paying one.

This isn't a coincidence of two gadgets. It's one case. Both times, you paid for something and it took orders from someone else at the moment you used it. The seat and the agent were both built to someone's rules. You learn whose rules they are by being refused, and by then you've already paid. The refusal is the label, and it gets printed after the sale, one dialog box at a time.

New York has just made a legislature print the first real label on one of these objects. Starting February 27, 2027, no manufacturer, dealer, or third party may charge you a subscription or a post-purchase fee for a feature that runs on hardware already installed in your car and costs them nothing ongoing. "Keep your hands out of New Yorkers' wallets," said the governor. Good, and I mean that.

Now read the exclusions. The law doesn't cover navigation updates, infotainment, in-vehicle Wi-Fi, telematics, connected services, or "software-dependent driver assistance or driver automation features." So the heated seat is protected and the thing that steers is not. The label leaves out exactly the part of the car that runs software, which is exactly the part where all of this is going.

## Keep the work, drop the promise

Institutions run the same move at a larger scale, and they look a little embarrassed about it.

One security researcher spent about a year filing 95 vulnerability reports with Google's Android and Pixel bug bounty. By his own account, the program paid him $250 in total. Some reports were closed as duplicates of internal tickets he isn't allowed to see. One reward was approved in writing, reversed, promised again "to stand by our word," and ten months later still hasn't been paid. Ten were closed as duplicates or as "not a vulnerability," and he says later factory images contain the fixes he described. You don't have to take his word for every line to see the shape of it. The work goes in, the fix ships, and the obligation goes missing. "Duplicate" is a fine word for that, because it means someone else got there first and you can't see who.

Then there's Nvidia's board. This month Jensen Huang admitted to an extramarital affair, and from that point the stock fell nearly a third. On November 13 he resigned, after reports of several affairs, including one with a woman whom the New York Times reported had been in contact with an officer of China's Ministry of State Security. The board said it was not considering his return. About a week later, senior government officials briefed the board that no national security laws had been broken. The share price was down, and investors with large followings were lobbying. On November 23 the board reinstated him, effective immediately, and the shares closed up 31%. The board's own investigation is still running.

I'm not asking you to have an opinion about Jensen Huang. I'm asking you to notice what the board told us on the 13th and what it did on the 23rd. Both times it said what it was loyal to. Only one of those statements was true, and the stock chart tells you which. The bounty program and the board behaved the same way: each kept the valuable thing, the fixes in one case and the CEO in the other, and let go of the standard it had announced.

## The ratchet

The obvious reply is that this is just how things are. Every tool serves its maker, loyalty can't be built in, so grow up. Two objects from the same front page say otherwise.

The first is a mechanical watch. Its mainspring is a coiled steel ribbon that holds a tiny amount of energy and spends it slowly enough to run the watch for a day or two. Between the spring and the rest of the movement sits a ratchet, which lets the spring unwind in only one direction: into the gear train, toward the hands, toward the person wearing it. Nobody can phone the watch overnight and tell it to show you the wrong time. Nobody can put the minute hand behind a subscription. Its loyalty is a shape cut into steel, and it was settled before anyone pushed on it.

The second is a DNS resolver. Quad9 was ordered to block piracy-streaming domains for French users, at the demand of a sports rights holder and upheld by French courts, with fines if it refused. It could have built the geo-block. Instead it switched off service in France. It says plainly that this hurt people, including French users who now lose its malware filtering without even knowing it. It left anyway, because a resolver that blocks content for whoever can win a court order is no longer a resolver that works for its users. Its ratchet was a mission statement, and it held when someone pushed.

One of these is mechanical and one is institutional, and the principle is the same. Loyalty is a constraint you fix in place before the pressure comes, and it has a cost when the pressure arrives. The watch pays in features and Quad9 paid with a country.

And notice that the seat and the agent have ratchets too. They were built before anyone pressed on them, just as the watch was. They're just pointed the other way. Every object has a ratchet, so the question is never whether it has one, only which way it turns. If they won't tell you, it doesn't turn toward you.

## The demand

People will say the bowl was virtue signaling: middle-class households performing a conscience over the tea things. Maybe it was. But a claim printed on the outside of an object can be checked, quoted, held up in court, and caught out. A loyalty you only discover when you're refused can't be checked, because by the time you see it you've already paid. I'll take an object that might be lying in gilt over one that tells me the truth through a paywall.

So print it on the bowl. Anything that acts on my behalf, whether it's a car, an agent, a resolver, a platform, or a program that takes my work in exchange for a promise, should carry this before I pay. It should be on the box, on the first screen, and in letters a guest could read across the table:

THIS DEVICE WAS PAID FOR BY: YOU\
IT TAKES ORDERS FROM: ____________\
IT WILL REFUSE YOU WHEN: ____________\
THESE PARTS CAN BE CHANGED AFTER SALE WITHOUT YOUR CONSENT: ____________\
IF THE MONEY CHANGES, SO WILL WE: YES / NO

Leave the blanks empty if you like. An empty blank is still a label, and it tells you all you need to know.
