The flag came up at 11:20, which was late for one, and Doyle read the summary out loud the way he read everything out loud, in the voice of a man reading a parts list off a box.

"Coordinated inauthentic behavior. Cluster of nineteen. Confidence point eight one."

"Where," Bex said.

"Podcast comments."

"We have podcast comments?"

"We have podcast comments," Doyle said, and turned his monitor forty degrees so she could see them, which he did roughly once a night and which always took him about eight seconds, because he moved the stand rather than the screen.

Nobody used the podcast comments. That was the whole reason the flag had fired. The episode was public radio, forty-one minutes, an interview about the Epic of Gilgamesh, and for two years it had carried three comments, one of which was a person asking how to cancel their subscription. It now had fourteen hundred.

"Okay, that's a botnet," Bex said.

"That's what the model says."

"I'm agreeing with the model. I'm allowed to agree with the model."

Something on the far monitor went amber, quarantined, and went green again. Neither of them looked up.

She started at the top of the cluster. The account names were the first thing, and they were genuinely a problem, because they had been run through a character generator: fullwidth letters, letters with rings over them, one that was mostly the same four emoji repeated until the field gave up. Doyle's tooling normalized them into a column of identical gray boxes, which was, he said, the correct behavior, and which meant that for the first four minutes neither of them could tell the nineteen accounts apart.

"I got called a bot last week," Bex said.

"On here?"

"No, in real life, on a forum, like a person. I said something was meaningful."

"You said something was meaningful."

"I said a repo was a meaningful contribution. And a guy replies, one line, that's Claude. That's a Claude word. And I'm sitting there going, what's the human word for meaningful."

"Significant," Doyle said.

"Significant is worse. Significant is so much worse."

He scrolled. She scrolled.

"My brother in law had that," he said.

"Had what."

"Called a chatbot. Woodworking forum. He wrote a post about a bandsaw, this was maybe four months ago, and somebody said it read like AI, so Ron pasted in eleven years of his post history, going all the way back, including a thread in 2014 where he asked whether he should buy the fourteen inch or the eighteen inch, and about forty people told him fourteen, and he bought the eighteen."

"And?"

"And the guy said that's exactly what a chatbot would do."

Bex laughed once, through her nose. "See, I lost mine before that. I typed a thing into search. I was trying to find where an old image macro came from, the caption was nobody is coming to help you, and I put it in with quotes and everything, and the overview at the top said it was sorry I was going through this."

"Going through what."

"Nothing. That's the point, Doyle. Nothing was happening to me. I was having a normal Tuesday and a search engine got worried about me and gave me a phone number."

"Did you call it?"

"No, I did not call it, I found the image macro, it's from a cat."

The comments, read in bulk, were as bad as the usernames. *hi.* *HIIII.* *ok M-H.* *your cats are so cute.* *gilgamesh quit and im crying.* Four hundred of them were a single exclamation point, or a run of them, in counts that did not appear to mean anything. The sentiment classifier had thrown its hands up and marked the whole cluster neutral, which was technically the least wrong thing anyone had said all night.

"Content is non-responsive to the media object," Doyle read.

"Content is non-responsive to everything. Content is non-responsive to itself."

"Activity is periodic." He tapped the histogram with a pen, which did nothing to the histogram. "That's your signature, right there. Consistent onset, consistent duration. That's a scheduled process."

Bex looked at the histogram.

The activity started at 3:40 in the afternoon and stopped at 4:20. Every weekday. Nothing on Saturday. Nothing on Sunday. Nothing at all for six weeks in July and August, and then, on a Tuesday in September, all of it at once, more than any week before it.

"That's a school bus," she said.

"That's a cron job."

"Doyle, that's a school bus and then a kitchen."

He did not argue, because Doyle did not argue, he simply kept his cursor where it was and waited for the argument to be made, and she made it by opening the playlists.

There were six. They were public, they had no description, and each of them contained exactly one item, which was the Gilgamesh episode. They were named *lets chat here*, and *lets chat here 2*, and *chat 2 REAL*, and *dont use the other one*, and *ok this one*, and *FINAL*.

"Oh my god," Bex said.

"It's an address book."

"It's a *clubhouse*." She was actually delighted, she could hear herself being delighted, and it did not occur to her to stop. "They can't DM. There's no DM on the product. So they made an address book out of the only thing on the account their parents let them have, and the address book is a playlist, and the room is a comment section nobody has opened since 2024."

The last artifact in the cluster summary was an outbound link, posted eleven times by six accounts, to an external form. Doyle read the title of it out loud in his parts list voice.

"Only cool people can fill this out."

Bex laughed, and then she stopped laughing, and the stopping took about a second and a half and happened for no reason that she could have given at the time.

When she was twelve, the homework portal her school used had a box under each assignment called *message to instructor*. It had no character limit, it did not log you out, and it kept everything you typed even after the assignment closed. Seven of them had lived in there for most of a year. It had never occurred to any of them that it was clever. It was simply the only door that had been left open, and so they had gone through it, the way water does.

"Doyle."

"Mm."

"I'm taking it."

He did not mind. Doyle's rate was fine; Doyle's rate had been fine for ten years. He moved his monitor forty degrees back, in eight seconds, and started on the next item, which was a man in Ohio with forty accounts and a genuine grievance.

Bex opened the disposition panel. The dropdown was already set, because the model had set it, to Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior. She changed it to Human, Off Policy, Accounts Likely Minor, and the panel expanded the way it always did, and showed her the field.

*Basis for reclassification. Describe the behavioral pattern in sufficient detail for reviewer confirmation. Required.*

So she described it. She was good at this, which was why they had her on nights. She wrote that the cluster used single item playlists with instructional titles as an out of band directory, that the titles superseded one another in sequence and the most recent title should be treated as the current address, that the host media object was selected for low traffic rather than for content and that content relevance was therefore not a useful signal, that activity was confined to 15:40 through 16:20 local on weekdays with total absence across the summer recess, and that entry to the cluster appeared to be gated by an external form.

It came to four sentences. It was the clearest thing she had written all month.

Under the field there was a checkbox, and the checkbox was checked, the way it came, the way it came on every item.

*Contribute to classifier feedback.*

She submitted it at 12:04. Something on the far monitor went amber, quarantined, and went green again.

Her handle time for the item was four minutes under target.
