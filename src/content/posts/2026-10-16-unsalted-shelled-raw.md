The peanuts have to be unsalted, shelled and raw. Not roasted. Roasting is a confound.

Martin Pell buys them in ten pound bags from the restaurant supply on Delacourt, where the man at the register stopped asking some years ago. He started with whatever was in the cupboard, which is a thing he has since written down in a four page document titled *Methodological Errors, 2015 to 2017*, along with the salted period, the almond period, and the eleven weeks he put the dish on the ground.

The dish is a white enamel pie plate, nine inches, on the northwest fence post. He sets it at 6:40 and is inside by 6:44. The crows come off the Hegg's transmission tower at about 7:20, which gives them thirty-six minutes to watch him leave, because they do watch him leave, and for two years he stood at the post and waited and understood later that standing there was a form of asking. There is a column in the spreadsheet for whether he spoke. He does not speak.

The principle he works from is simple enough that he has never had to write it down. A gift cannot be requested. It has to arrive on its own. But things that arrive on their own still arrive for reasons, and reasons can be supplied, and so the entire problem is one of supplying the reasons without appearing to supply anything. He has been getting better at this for eleven years.

Audra Fenn moved into the house on the other side of the chain link four years ago last spring.

On her kitchen windowsill there is a jam jar with a bottle cap in it, a blue glass bead, two inches of solder, a flake of mirror, and the small curved bone of something. The bead came first. The bead came in her second summer.

"What do you put out," he asked her once, over the fence, in a voice he had practised to sound incidental.

"Cat food," she said. "Whatever's going. Sometimes the ends of things."

He wrote *cat food, irregular, no schedule* in the notebook that evening and sat with it a long while. The ends of things. He has considered, seriously, running a parallel site with an unscheduled irregular provision of the ends of things, and has not, because he cannot do it without knowing he is doing it, and a thing done knowingly is not irregular. It is a fourth protocol wearing the costume of no protocol. Audra has no protocol. That is her only advantage and it is not transferable.

"What did you do," he asked her, the year the bone appeared. "Before the first one. Did anything change."

She thought about it in the way people think about a question they have no reason to have thought about before. "I don't think I did anything," she said.

He wrote that down too.

The trail camera went up in 2019, and the weather housing for it in 2021, and the second camera in 2023 to cover the approach from the maple. He ran a control week in 2022, seven days with no dish at all, which he found much harder than he expected and logged as *Control, compliance 7/7* without further comment. He named them at first, Notch and Half and Grey Tail and Six, and abandoned the names in 2024 when he admitted in writing that he could not reliably distinguish individuals and never could have, and that every entry attributing behaviour to a specific bird was worthless. He did not delete those entries. He flagged them. There is a limitations section.

In March he posted all of it. Two thousand eight hundred and forty photographs, the full spreadsheet, the errors document, the limitations, under the title *Eleven Years of Corvid Provisioning: Null Result*. He was proud of the honesty of the title.

A man called kettlebrook replied on the third day.

They do not give gifts, kettlebrook wrote. A crow carrying a bright object and arriving at food has to put the object down to eat. Sometimes it puts it down near the food. Sometimes the food is near a person. The placement is not a placement, it is a bird setting down something it is finished carrying, and the gift is a thing you add afterward, in your own head, standing at a fence. He went further, which is the part Martin read four times. The thing you want has never once happened, to anybody. Not to your neighbour either. What she has in that jar is five pieces of litter and a story, and the story is the part she likes, and you have spent eleven years trying to earn your way into a story. There is nothing there. There was never anything there. You have the most complete record anyone has ever assembled of a thing that does not exist.

Martin's reply ran to nine hundred words and concerned the inferential weakness of a null result from a single unblinded site with no matched control, and closed by noting, politely, that the adjacent property had produced five positive events in four years under a protocol so loose that nobody had bothered to record it, and that any account of the phenomenon which could not explain the adjacent property was not an account of anything. He added a column that week. *Objects observed within two metres of dish.* It takes a zero every morning. He enters the zero himself rather than letting the sheet default, because a default is not an observation.

In June a man called wrenlowe wrote to say his father had fed one crow for eleven years.

The same crow, he thought, or they always assumed so. It stood in a particular spot in the gravel by the back step, and it would only stand there if it could see his father through the kitchen window, and it came three mornings a week on no schedule anyone could find. In the last years it brought a fledgling and stood with it in the gravel, visibly and repeatedly showing the fledgling how to stand there and be seen. The fledgling never got it. His father died in February. Nothing was ever brought. Not once, in eleven years. He thought Martin would want to know that somebody else had the same nothing.

Martin wrote back the same evening to ask what the father had used, whether it was shelled, whether the gravel had been raked and on what interval, what time the window was occupied, and whether any of it had been written down. Wrenlowe answered that his father had used supermarket peanuts and that nobody had measured anything and that he was sorry, he had only meant it as a kind of company. Martin read this as the explanation for the absence of findings and entered the site in his notes as *unusable, second-hand, no instrumentation*. It was the longest duration he had ever heard of besides his own. He was glad to have it.

On the third of April he lost the brass screw.

It came out of the lower bracket of the weather housing while he was remounting it, and went into the grass below the post, and he spent forty minutes down there with a magnet taped to a dowel and did not find it. He replaced it with a stainless one from the jar in the garage. The entry reads: *Housing screw, brass, 6mm, slotted, lost in grass below NW post. Substituted stainless. Does not match.* He had painted the brass one grey with the rest of the housing in 2021, so there was a fleck of grey on the head, and he noted that too, because he notes things.

On Tuesday morning there is something on the fence post, two inches from the dish.

It is brass, 6mm, slotted, with grey paint on the head.

He does not pick it up for nine minutes. He photographs it in place from four angles against the ruler, and then on the gridded mat indoors, and weighs it on the jeweller's scale at 0.41 grams, and compares all of it against the April entry, and there is no question. It is his screw. It came out of his housing and went into his grass in April and it is now on top of his post in October.

He pulls the card.

At 6:41 the post is bare. At 7:12 the screw is there. Between those frames the camera has fired fourteen times and in eleven of them there is a bird, a wing, a tail, a foot on the rim of the dish, and in not one of them is there a beak at the post. Because the camera is aimed at the dish. He aimed it at the dish in 2019 and corrected it twice and both times he corrected it toward the dish, and the top of the post, the flat grey two inches where a thing could be set down, has been outside the frame for seven years.

He goes through all fourteen again at full resolution. Then the second camera, which covers the maple and shows him nothing but the maple.

So he has an object that is definitely his and no record of how it got up there. It was in the grass. Grass below the post. A bird that was down in the grass and came up to the rim of the dish would have come up with whatever it was holding, and would have had to put it down somewhere to eat, and the nearest flat surface to the rim of the dish is the top of the post.

Or it was carried up and set down there.

Those are not the same thing and he cannot tell them apart and there is no column for either of them. He makes one. He types *Received* in the header and deletes it. He types *Returned* and deletes that. He leaves the header blank and enters the date in the row beneath it.

Audra is in her yard in the afternoon, doing something to a hose.

"You got one," she says, seeing him at the fence with it.

"It's mine," he says. "It's my screw. It came off the camera in April."

She comes over and looks at it in his palm, and he waits for her to say the thing that settles it, that she knows, that hers were never anyone's, that there is a difference and she can name it. "Huh," she says, and goes back to the hose.

He cleans it with a toothbrush and alcohol and photographs it clean, both faces, and weighs it again at 0.41 grams.

Then he goes out and loosens the camera bracket and swings it two degrees down and one inch left, so that the top of the post sits dead centre in the frame, and tightens it, and checks the preview on his phone, and tightens it again.

In the morning he sets out the dish at 6:40. Then he sets the screw on the post beside it, in the middle, where the light is good, and goes inside, and watches.
