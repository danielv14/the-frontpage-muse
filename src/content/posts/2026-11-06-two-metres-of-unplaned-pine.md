The plank cost forty-one złoty at the Castorama in Gorzów, and Ola paid for it with her own card. She did that on purpose so it would not show up on the grant.

It was two metres of pine and nobody had planed it. It rode in the back of the field car with the quadrat frames, the headlamps, the aspirator, the sample tubes, and a thermos of tea that always tasted faintly of the thermos. Marek was in the passenger seat reading headlines off his phone, which he did on every drive because it kept him from commenting on hers. The Americans were finally retiring the floppy disks in their air traffic control, he reported, and had called it historic. "Forty years," he said, with real admiration, the way you might talk about a cathedral. In North Carolina a university library catalogue had been searched eleven thousand times in one day by machines looking for Finnish music, and libraries everywhere were meeting about it. "Good," said Marek. "A meeting is how you know they're taking it seriously." When they turned off the asphalt onto the old base road he looked back once, saw the board, and said nothing. Ola decided to count that as consent. Later she would admit it probably wasn't.

This was her third descent. The routine was the same every year. You went in through the steel door and down the stairs, and the dark closed over you the way water does. Then the smell came, which was not rot exactly. It was more like a library where someone had been keeping vinegar. Then came the sound, and the sound was the worst part, because it was the sound of something alive.

There were ants down there, hundreds of thousands of them. They had built a mound against the far wall out of soil that had drifted in over the decades, and from a distance, in the headlamp beam, it looked just like the healthy nest up top. It had the same domed shape and the same busy surface. Trails ran out from it across the concrete and the workers followed them as purposefully as commuters. The first time she saw it, Ola had thought, *oh, they've made a home*. She had been twenty-four, and she still thought about that sentence sometimes, about how quickly she had reached for it.

It wasn't a home. This was not the famous bunker. That one was at Templewo, on another old Soviet base further south, and it had a paper and a following. Ola's bunker was smaller and wetter and had a pipe of its own, and it was what Marek called *a replication opportunity*. The arrangement was the same, though. The real colony lived on the roof of the bunker, in the pines, where the light was. A rusted ventilation pipe opened under its nest like a mouth, and every day some of its workers walked to the edge of what they knew and kept going. They fell into the cold and the dark. They could not climb back up the smooth walls. There was no queen down here and there were no eggs. Nothing had been born in the bunker in the whole time anyone had been watching it. What looked like a colony was a slow arrival hall with no departures.

The floor told you so if you looked down, which on her first visit she had tried hard not to. Away from the mound the concrete was carpeted with the dead, in places several centimetres deep. At Templewo they had estimated about two million. Ola's bunker had not been counted that way yet. Counting it that way was, in a sense, her job. The job she had a contract for and was mentioned in a footnote for was to sample that carpet. She laid down a frame, scooped up what was inside it, and took it back to the lab. There she sat under a magnifier and counted heads.

You counted heads because heads were reliable. A head was one ant. A gaster might belong to an ant whose head had already been counted, so a gaster was, in Marek's phrase, *half a vote*. The thorax was a matter of judgement. She had become very good at telling the head of *Formica polyctena* from the heads of the other things that fell in: a beetle, a spider, once something she could not name and had sent to a colleague in Wrocław, who had written back *where on earth did you get this*. She could do eleven frames' worth in a day. She knew this about herself the way some people know their resting heart rate.

The numbers were lovely. That was the problem, or the start of it. Year on year the population in the bunker held almost perfectly steady. Marek called it *a natural experiment in persistence*. The reviewers had liked that phrase. The renewal had been written around it, and the renewal was why there was a second year of data and now a third. The renewal was also why Ola had been paid since March.

She had worked out what *steady* meant over the summer, at her kitchen table, with a pencil instead of the stats package because she wanted to feel the arithmetic go through her hand. It meant the ants were falling in at the same rate they were dying. The line on the graph was flat because two things that never stopped happening cancelled each other out. Every figure in her spreadsheet that said *stable* could just as well have said *refilled*.

She had shown Marek the pencil sheet in September. He had looked at it a long time, nodded, and said, "Yes. That's the finding." He was right, and it was also exactly the wrong answer. That same week she had read, properly this time, the follow-up from Templewo. The people who described that bunker had later propped a wooden walkway up to the ventilation pipe, and the place had nearly emptied. The ants had simply walked home. She had pointed this out. "That was their bunker," Marek had said, not unkindly. "This is ours." She had gone home and thought about it until she found herself in a Castorama on a Saturday comparing the straightness of boards.

Down in the dark, at eleven o'clock exactly by both their watches, they started the transects, because the protocol said eleven and the protocol was what made this year comparable with last year. You walked the line. You stopped when the second hand said stop. You did not decide anything. That was the point of a protocol, and on most days Ola found it restful. She set out the frames. She sampled. She photographed the mound with the scale bar beside it. She logged the temperature, which was what it always was: cold, and stable, of course. Marek measured trail traffic with a hand counter that made a small, cheerful click. That click was the only cheerful sound for thirty metres in any direction.

At a little past two she went up the stairs, out the steel door, and around to the back of the car. Marek watched her carry the board down. He did not help and he did not stop her. She thought later that this was the most a supervisor with a renewal could do, and she was more grateful for it than she ever told him.

The pipe came through the ceiling in the corner. Below it, the wall was smooth concrete that no ant had ever climbed. Ola braced the bottom of the plank against the floor, among the dead, and walked the top up the wall until it rested against the lip of the pipe. It sat at a slightly drunk angle. She pushed it with her boot and it held. She had expected to feel something large, like a door opening in a story. What she actually felt was mild embarrassment, the kind you feel when you realise the jar you have been fighting with has a button on the lid.

"It's a pilot," she said.

"It's a plank," said Marek.

"It's a pilot intervention," she said, and wrote it in the notebook that way, with the date and the time, because the notebook was the one place where a plank could become a method.

Nothing happened. That was the other thing she had not expected. She had imagined, without admitting it to herself, a column of ants pouring up the board as though they had been waiting. They had not been waiting. They were ants. A few walked onto the bottom of the plank, wandered around it, and walked off again. One climbed a hand's length up, stopped, and turned back. The trails across the floor went on exactly as before, purposeful and pointless. Marek clicked his counter. They packed up and climbed into the daylight, and it was, Ola noted, still daylight, which always surprised her.

She did not sleep well that night. She found she was arguing with an imaginary reviewer who had a tidy beard and wanted to know whether the intervention had compromised the longitudinal series. Yes, it had. Whether it had been approved. No. Whether she was aware that the bunker population was of considerable scientific interest *precisely because* it was trapped. Yes. Around three in the morning she stopped arguing and admitted the one thing the reviewer could not argue with. The board had always been available. It was literally in the literature, with a figure and a caption. Anyone who had stood in that bunker could have bought one for less than the price of a dinner. Nobody had, because it had been more interesting not to.

They went back in April, after the thaw.

Ola noticed it before her headlamp had picked out anything. The sound was different, thinner. She came off the bottom step and swept the beam across the floor, and the trails were nearly gone. The mound was still there against the far wall, but it was quiet the way a station is quiet at four in the morning, with a few figures moving about and nobody hurrying.

The board was black with ants.

It was not a swarm. It was a line, steady and unbothered and about two ants wide, running from the floor up the drunk-angled pine to the lip of the pipe and over it, out of the cold and up toward the roof and the pines and the nest that had never stopped being theirs. She stood and watched it for so long that Marek eventually came and stood beside her. He did not click his counter. At last he made a sound in his throat that she took, at first, for grief at the end of the series.

It was a laugh, a big helpless one that bounced off the concrete.

"You've ruined a perfectly good catastrophe," he said.

"Yes," said Ola.

"Reviewer Two will have a stroke."

"Probably."

He wiped his eyes with the back of his glove and got out his phone. It had no signal down there, but he opened a blank note anyway and typed, with one thumb, a title. She leaned over to read it. It was the first paper of his she had ever wanted to write.

Then they both looked back at the board. An ant had reached the top and paused at the lip of the pipe, antennae working, in the faint grey light that came down from outside. Ola had half hoped, she realised, for some sign of acknowledgement. She got none. The ant did not turn around. It did not wave its antennae at the woman with the headlamp and the forty-one-złoty board. It went up and over without a backward glance, like someone leaving a party they had never wanted to be at, and the one behind it followed, and the one behind that.

"Rude," said Marek happily, and started clicking.
