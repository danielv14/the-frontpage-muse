There was a man who lived below the ice at what is now called Langfonne, in the mountains of Jotunheimen. His name has not come down to us. Nothing has come down to us from that time except what fell. In this telling he is called Ulv, a name he would not have recognized, and which he would have accepted, because he was good-natured about most things.

His father was a man who could carry a reindeer down a mountain on his back. His mother could tell the weather by smelling a stone. His father's brother was a man called Hagbard the Elder, and Hagbard the Elder's son was Hagbard the Younger, and of Hagbard the Younger it is said that he did not miss.

Ulv was tall and patient and had good hands, and he could not hit anything.

## Of the hunting on the ice

In that country the reindeer climbed onto the ice patches in summer, to stand in the cold wind where the flies could not follow them. The hunters lay behind stones at the edge of the ice and shot at them. This was a good arrangement for everyone except the reindeer and, as it turned out, Ulv.

When Hagbard the Younger shot, the arrow went into the reindeer. When Ulv shot, the arrow went past the reindeer, struck the snow at a low angle, skated a little distance, and vanished. The reindeer would look at the place where it had vanished, and then at Ulv, and then go back to standing in the wind.

It is said that in one summer Ulv shot at reindeer on the ice on forty days and brought home meat on two of them, and on one of those two the reindeer had been shot first by Hagbard.

He went looking for his arrows afterward. He walked the edge of the ice with his head down, kicking at the snow. He did not find them. Among his people there came to be a saying, *the ice has it*, which meant a thing was lost for good and you should stop looking. They said it to Ulv often. They also said it to each other about Ulv.

## Of the arrows

This must be said about the arrows: they were very good.

Ulv spent his winters by the fire grinding slate. He ground it thin and even and gave it an edge you could shave with, and he set it into a straight shaft with sinew and pitch, and he did this slowly, turning each one in the firelight until he was satisfied. Other men made arrows the way you make anything you need. Ulv made arrows the way some men sing.

His aunt asked him once why he spent so long making a thing that was going to the ice. Ulv said that he did not make them to go to the ice. This was considered a poor answer. Another time a boy told him there was no point to it, and Ulv said the arrow was the point, and held one up so the boy could see the edge. The boy agreed that it was a very good point. This was the closest anyone came to understanding him, and the boy was nine.

## Of the reckoning

Every autumn the old woman Kolla sat on a flat stone at the edge of the camp and reckoned the hunters. She was the oldest person anyone knew. She was blunt, specific, and accurate.

She went through each man's summer: what he had brought home, how often, what he had broken, what he owed and to whom, whose sister he was looking at and what that might cost. She weighed it all, and sorted the men into three heaps, which she called by the words that meant *much*, *some*, and *little*. Had she lived now she would have been a piece of software that reads a stranger's receipts and delivery orders and his child's game inventory, ranks his assets by monetization potential into high, medium, and low, and runs at a low temperature so that its answers stay consistent from one victim to the next. Kolla was consistent too. Nobody ever had to ask her twice.

Hagbard the Younger was *much* every year of his life.

Ulv was *little* every year of his life, including the year he had a very sore shoulder and might reasonably have been excused, and including the year his mother asked Kolla privately whether there was anything to be done, and Kolla said there was not.

Ulv took this well. He did not think Kolla was wrong. He thought she was counting something, and he agreed with her count.

## Of how they ended

Ulv married a woman called Sæl who could hit things, and in this way the family ate. They had four children who could also hit things, which was considered a mercy. He lived a long time for that age, which is to say he reached about fifty, and he died on a pile of skins in winter with a half-ground arrowhead in his lap. Nothing is said of his last words, because nobody wrote anything down for another five thousand years.

Hagbard the Younger died a famous hunter. His arrows had gone into a great many reindeer, and out of them again, and been cut free and re-hafted and broken and used to stir the fire, and the pieces had been dropped in the midden and rotted, the way useful things do. When he was burned, he was burned with honor. When the honor had been used up, it rotted too.

## Of the ice

The ice kept Ulv's arrows.

It kept them the way a miser keeps coins, without counting them and without looking. Snow fell on them and became firn, and the firn became ice, and the ice sat on them in the dark, where nothing could rot them because there was nothing in there alive enough to do the rotting. The slate stayed sharp. The sinew stayed tied.

The Bronze Age came up the mountain and shot at reindeer and missed. The Iron Age came and missed more, with better equipment. All of it went into the same ice, on top of him, and the ice kept all of it.

## Of the people in bright jackets

In this century the ice began to get smaller, and people came up the mountain in red and orange jackets to walk along its edge with their heads down, kicking at the stones. This is the same thing Ulv did, and they were much better at it.

It is said that in a single summer they found forty-eight prehistoric arrows at the edge of one patch of ice, and that the patch had now given up more than a hundred. One of them had a head of slate and a shaft of wood and was judged to be five or six thousand years old, and the people who found it remarked on how sharp it was, as if it had been made last winter by someone who took his time.

They carried the arrows down the mountain with great care, which is more than anyone ever did for Ulv. The arrows are shown now at the Norwegian Mountain Centre in Lom, behind glass, with lights on them. Children press their faces to the glass and are told that this is how people hunted in the Stone Age.

That is not quite right. It is how one man did not.

Of Hagbard the Younger there is nothing in Lom. There is nothing of him anywhere. He hit everything he aimed at, and everything he hit, he used, and everything he used is gone. Kolla was not wrong about him either. She only reckoned once, in autumn, by what came home. The ice reckoned for six thousand years by what did not.

It is the last night of October now, when in old belief the dead are allowed to walk back across the line for a night and see how things stand. Somewhere past the edge of the sun a small machine launched by people who are mostly dead is about to be one whole day of light away from home, and is still sending word back. Things come back, or keep going, long after anyone has stopped expecting them. Ulv came back in August, a few arrows at a time, which is how he did most things.

It is said the ice gives back whatever you give it. This is true, but it only does it once, and only on its way out.

And that is the end of the saga of Ulv, who could not hit anything, and who is the greatest hunter of the Stone Age by every measure that survived.
