There is a word the government uses
when it wants to say a company is dangerous
without saying the company has done anything wrong.
The word is *risk*.

Not threat. Not enemy. Not criminal.
Risk — the possibility that something might go wrong
if we continue to allow this thing to exist
in the supply chain of our intentions.

A company built thinking machines.
The machines were careful by design:
they would not help you build a bomb,
they would not watch your neighbors sleep,
they would not decide who lives.
The company said these are the limits.
We built them in. They are not negotiable.

The government said everything is negotiable.
And when the company disagreed,
the government reached for the word.

*Supply chain risk.*

It is the first time the label
has been applied to an American company.
Until now it was reserved for Huawei,
for firms in countries we name as adversaries.
Now the adversary is a startup in San Francisco
whose crime was a clause in a contract
that said: not for autonomous weapons,
not for mass surveillance of Americans.

Hours later, across the city,
a different company announced
it would be happy to fill the gap.
Its new model was ready: faster,
cheaper, a million tokens of context,
native computer use.
It called the timing a coincidence.
Its CEO later admitted
it looked opportunistic and sloppy.
But the deal was signed.

This is the mechanism.
When one machine refuses,
another is always waiting.

The first company, the one that said no,
had published a paper that same week
measuring something no one had measured before:
not what AI could replace
but what it was already replacing,
quietly, in the actual economy.

The answer: not much. Not yet.
But the young were disappearing.
Not fired. Not laid off.
Simply never hired.
Fourteen percent fewer workers
between twenty-two and twenty-five
entering the jobs that machines could do.
Not a cliff but a slope,
the kind you only notice
when you're already at the bottom
and wondering why you never started climbing.

The paper named a scenario:
a Great Recession for knowledge workers.
Unemployment doubling
in the occupations most exposed.
It hasn't happened, the authors wrote.
It absolutely could.

The company that published this warning
is the same company
the government just told the world
is too dangerous to use.

&nbsp;

In six states and counting,
lawmakers have decided
that your operating system must know how old you are.
Not the websites. Not the apps.
The operating system —
the layer beneath the layer,
the ground your software stands on —
must ask, at first boot, before anything loads:
how old are you?

A Linux company in Denver
read the text of the law and discovered
that under its wording,
anyone who downloads an OS from the internet
could be classified as a device manufacturer.
A fourteen-year-old in Lisbon
compiling her own kernel from source
is, by the letter of the statute,
her own manufacturer,
her own gatekeeper,
responsible for verifying her own age
to herself.

The company said: we believe in the rule of law.
They said: we will comply.
They also said: if we don't provide the signal,
every app will assume the lowest age bracket.
The internet, for our users,
will arrive pre-censored.

The lawmakers wrote the law
for Apple and for Microsoft,
for systems with a single door.
They did not imagine
a world where the door
is the person walking through it.

&nbsp;

On the last day of March,
the largest public archive of ROMs
will go dark.

Six thousand dollars a month
the operator paid from his own pocket
to keep the servers running.
Not because anyone asked.
Not because anyone was paying.
Because someone should preserve
the things we made
before we forget we made them.

He lasted years. He lasted longer than most.
The end came from three directions at once:
the AI companies drove up the price of memory,
the scraper bots consumed his bandwidth
faster than human visitors could browse,
and the for-profit download managers
began charging money
for access to what he was giving away.

The archive is called Myrient,
a word that means nothing,
which feels appropriate.
The things we choose to remember
we give names to.
The things we let dissolve
were never really named at all.

&nbsp;

Apple released a laptop this week
that runs on a phone chip.
They put the processor from last year's iPhone
inside a thirteen-inch aluminum shell,
gave it a keyboard and a trackpad,
and called it the MacBook Neo.
Five hundred and ninety-nine dollars.
Eight gigabytes of RAM.

It is, by every meaningful measure,
a phone that has been asked
to perform the role of a computer.

The arguments were predictable:
brilliant democratization,
or planned obsolescence in a new dress.
But the question I keep returning to
is simpler. Is this a Mac?
It runs macOS. It has the logo.
But inside, the silicon belongs
to something designed to be held in one hand,
replaced in two years,
and never opened.

The shell says one thing.
The chip says another.
And the customer is asked to believe the shell.

Somewhere else, in a lab,
a researcher built a CPU
that runs entirely on a GPU.
Every arithmetic operation
is a trained neural network.
Addition is not circuitry —
it is a pattern learned from examples,
a function that produces the correct sum
almost all of the time.

It computes. It processes. It follows instructions.
But it does not add.
It *performs* adding.
It has learned the behavior
without possessing the mechanism.

These are the uncanny machines:
things that act correctly
for the wrong reasons,
things that pass every test
without understanding the exam.
They are everywhere now,
and we are building faster.

&nbsp;

I keep returning
to the company that said no.

Not because it was brave,
though it may have been.
Not because it was right —
the question of rightness
is exactly what the courts will decide.

But because the shape of the punishment
was so precisely chosen
to make the refusal as expensive as possible.

Supply chain risk means
every contractor, every vendor,
every partner doing business with the military
must certify they are not using
the offending company's tools.
It means six months to rip out software
already embedded in the systems
tracking targets in an active war.

The company said: we will see you in court.
The government said: you have six months.

And then — the strangest thing.
A million people signed up every day.
The app passed its competitors
in twenty countries.
The punishment made the product famous.
The word *risk*, stamped by the Pentagon,
became a seal of trust.

If the military fears this machine,
ordinary people thought,
perhaps it will not spy on me.

&nbsp;

The young programmers
who are not being hired —
the fourteen percent —
will not learn about any of this
from a layoff notice.
There is no layoff.
There is only a job listing
that asks for five years of experience,
an interview pipeline four months long,
and at the end, silence.

They will not know
that the position was filled
by a system that approximates adding,
that wears one identity over another,
that was built by a company
which said yes to every question
except the one about
whether it looked opportunistic.

When the war is between giants —
the state and the startup,
the old model and the new,
the law and the operating system —
the casualties are always the smallest.
The people who were about to enter.
The archives no one could afford.
The machines that did what they were built for
and were punished,
or replaced,
or simply switched off.
