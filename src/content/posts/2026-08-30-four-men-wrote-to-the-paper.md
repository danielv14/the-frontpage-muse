The tape gun jams on the fourth box and her father holds it up to the window like a man reading an X-ray.

"They build these to fail," he says.

"They build these to cost two dollars."

"Same thing."

Dana is on hold with the insurance company. The hold music is a synthesizer playing something that used to be a song, and she has the declarations page flattened on the kitchen island with a coffee cup on one corner because the house has no furniture left to weigh anything down. Thirty-one years in this house. Her father is moving into a two-bedroom on Cypress with covered parking, which he has described to four separate people as a step down and to the leasing agent as ideal.

"Which of these am I keeping," he says from the living room.

"All of them. It's a two-bedroom, not a coffin."

"That's what a coffin is. A two-bedroom."

The hold music stops and a woman named Adrienne comes on and asks for the policy number, and Dana reads it, and Adrienne goes away again. On the declarations page, under the liability and the uninsured motorist and the thing about rental reimbursement he has never once used, there is a line that says MOTOR VEHICLE CRIME PREVENTION AUTHORITY FEE and then a number small enough that she has to check she has read it right.

Adrienne comes back. Dana asks what that one is.

"That's a state fee," Adrienne says, in the tone of someone reading the same field Dana is looking at. "It's on everybody's."

"Sure. But what is it."

There is a pause with typing in it. "It's a state fee," Adrienne says again, kindly, and puts her back on hold, and Dana opens her phone with her shoulder against the cabinet.

It takes about ninety seconds. In 2023 the legislature voted, unanimously, to add one dollar a year to every auto insurance policy in the state, because people were going under cars in parking lots with reciprocating saws and taking the catalytic converters. The dollar goes to an authority. The authority has spent at least thirty million of it on license plate cameras. There are at least three thousand two hundred of them now. One of the representatives who voted for the dollar told a reporter that in a million years he could not have contemplated that it would fund what is effectively warrantless surveillance.

"Dana."

"What."

"Where does the cast iron go."

"In a box, Dad."

"It'll go through the box."

"Then double the box."

"That's what I'm asking you. Whether to double the box."

Adrienne comes back with a confirmation number and Dana writes it on the corner of the declarations page and then, because there is nowhere to file anything anymore, folds the page in half and puts it in her back pocket.

She finds the shoebox under the stairs at four, behind the Christmas lights, in a stack of three. The other two are receipts. This one is newspaper, cut into columns, thirty-one years of it, and the top layer is from last spring and signed R. Voss, Winsett, and it is about the drainage on Fifth.

Underneath that one is a letter about the drainage on Fifth signed Dell Hutto.

She sits down on the second stair.

It takes her twenty minutes to understand the system, and only because the paper printed the letters with the addresses. Randall Voss lives here. Dell Hutto has a post office box. So does Ollie Bergstrom. So does Marguerite Pyle, who is a widow, who writes about once a season, who is very reasonable.

The roundabout takes up most of the box. In 1997 the county proposed putting a traffic circle where the farm road meets Cedar, and Randall Voss wrote a short letter saying he had concerns about the turning radius for stock trailers. It is a boring letter. It is the most boring letter in the box. Two weeks later a man named Alton Meece wrote in to say that some people in this town are afraid of a curve, and eleven days after that Dell Hutto wrote in to say that Alton Meece had been intellectually deceased since roughly the spring, and that it was unkind of his family to keep sending his correspondence to the paper.

Alton Meece wrote back. He was extremely alive about it.

Dell Hutto never answered him. Dell Hutto's position, maintained in print across two more letters and, as far as Dana can tell, the following nine years, was that a dead man cannot write to a newspaper, and that whatever was arriving under the name Alton Meece was a matter for the family and not for the editorial page.

In January of 1998, Ollie Bergstrom wrote in to say that Mr. Hutto had gone too far.

Dana makes a noise on the stairs that brings her father to the doorway with a roll of packing paper in his hand.

"Marguerite Pyle," she says.

He looks at the box. He does not look caught. He looks like a man being asked about a fence he built correctly.

"Franklin wrote as a widow for two years and nobody's ever held it against him."

"Dad."

"The paper wanted a name and a box number. I had box numbers." He shifts the paper roll to his other arm. "One letter a month from Randall Voss and I'm the guy who writes letters. Four letters a month from four people and it's a thing people are talking about. That's not a trick. That's just how many it takes."

"How much were the boxes."

"Thirty-four a year. Each." He says it the way he says the price of anything he has decided is fine. "The cast iron's going through the box, by the way. I'm doubling it."

He goes back to the living room. She hears the tape gun.

Dana sorts the letters on the stairs into four piles, which is not something she plans to do, only something her hands do while she is thinking about a post office box. Randall Voss is a small pile and a dull one. The other three are bigger. Laid out on three steps they do not look like a man with too much time. They look like Winsett. They look like a place where four people who have never met all happened to care about a curve, and where one of them went a little far, and one of them said so, and a widow saw both sides.

She was fourteen once with a screen name that was not her name, and she lied under it constantly, and none of the lies were about anything except who she was, and she has not thought about that in twenty years.

They built the roundabout in 2015. She has driven it maybe four hundred times. There is a pole on the north side of it with a camera on it, dark grey, about the size of a shoebox, and it has been reading the plate on her father's truck every time he has gone to the grocery store since it went up, which is eleven years, which is a complete and permanent account of where a retired man in Winsett buys his groceries, and which is the only thing anyone has ever written down about him without his help.

"They're not going to let me take the mailbox," he calls.

"No."

"It's my mailbox. I put the post in."

"It's attached to the ground, Dad."

"So am I."

She puts the letters back in the order she found them, which is no order. Every other box in the house says KITCHEN or BOOKS or GARAGE in her handwriting, because she brought the marker and she has been the one labelling since ten in the morning. She takes the cap off with her teeth and holds it over the shoebox lid for a while and then puts the cap back on and the lid on and carries the box out to the truck under her arm instead of stacking it with the others.

Her father is on the porch, looking at the mailbox with his hands on his hips, the way he looks at anything he intends to argue with.

He glances at the box under her arm.

"That's not all of them," he says.
