The first chirp came at 2:51 a.m. Dana knew the time because she lay there waiting for the second one, and when it came she looked at her phone to see how long she had to live like this. Fifty-eight seconds.

It wasn't her smoke detector. The apartment's detector was hardwired, a white saucer on the hall ceiling with a green light that had never once looked at her. This came from the spare room, where forty-one boxes from her father's house were stacked to shoulder height. They had been there since October, still taped.

She stood in the doorway in socks and waited. Chirp. It came from everywhere, which is how those things work. They pitch the sound high, and a high, short sound bounces off every wall until it seems to come from inside your own skull.

Her brother Kevin was in Denver and still awake, because Kevin was always still awake. She texted him. He sent back a link inside a minute.

*Triangulates sounds using multiple devices. Guy found a beeping thing in his garage with it. Super cool.*

She opened it. It wanted three devices, ideally four, placed around the room at measured distances. It wanted her to make an account. It wanted to sync to a server so the devices could agree on what time it was. She owned one phone. The laptop was at work. The server, when she tapped "Get Started," returned a spinning circle, and then a polite sentence about high demand.

Chirp.

She called her father.

He picked up on the first ring, the way he always had, as if the phone were something he'd been holding in his lap. He was at Linden Court now, in a room with a call button, a window onto the parking lot, and a fall sensor in the ceiling that he said could tell from the Wi-Fi when he got up to pee. So at night he mostly didn't get up. He sat in the chair and listened to the radio.

"It's three in the morning," he said, by way of hello.

"I know. Something in your boxes is beeping."

"Hold the phone up."

She held it toward the stacks. Chirp.

"That's the kitchen CO detector," he said. "Low battery. One chirp a minute is the battery. If it were five in a row it'd be telling you it's dead, and you'd throw it out. That one's fine. It just wants a battery."

"Kevin sent me an app."

"Does it work?"

"It needs four phones and a server."

"Then no." She could hear him getting comfortable, the chair creaking, the radio going down. "Move half the boxes into the hall."

"Dad."

"Half into the hall. Then stand in the hall and listen. Whichever pile is chirping, that's the whole world now. The other pile doesn't exist. You don't carry anything back."

She put him on speaker and set the phone on the floor.

Forty-one is an awkward number, so she moved twenty. The first box she picked up said KITCHEN MISC in his block capitals. It weighed about as much as she did, and she put it straight back down.

"What's in KITCHEN MISC?"

"The skillets."

"Why are the skillets misc?"

"Because they're not dishes and they're not pans. Pans have lids." He said this the way he had said everything about the house, as if the categories had been handed down on stone. "Move the light ones. It's never the heavy box. Nobody packs a smoke detector with the cast iron."

"You don't know that."

"I packed it."

So she moved the light ones: DEN, LINENS, BATHROOM, PAPERS (KEEP), PAPERS (?). Twenty boxes into the hall, shoved against the wall so the neighbor below wouldn't hear her drag them. Then she stood between the two piles in the dark and waited out a minute.

Chirp. Behind her. The spare room.

"Room," she said.

"Good. Forget the hall. Now do it again."

Twenty-one boxes left. She moved ten into the hall, next to the first twenty, and stood there feeling like a woman who had bought a smaller apartment and was now furnishing its hallway with her father's life. Chirp. Hall.

"Hall," she said. "The new pile."

"See, now you're cooking. Ten boxes. What are they?"

She read the labels into the phone. Most said KITCHEN. One said KITCHEN DRAWER (JUNK), which made him laugh, a short dry sound like a stapler.

"Don't open the junk drawer," he said. "Not at three in the morning. You'll be up until six."

"I wasn't going to open anything."

"I know." He let that sit for a second, which wasn't like him. She hadn't opened a single box since October. He knew, because he'd asked twice whether she'd found his good flashlight, and twice she'd said she hadn't looked yet, which was true. The binary search had one thing going for it that the app didn't, and he was smart enough not to say it out loud: you could do the whole thing without looking inside anything until the very end.

She split the ten and carried five of them into her bedroom. "Any room," he said. "Doesn't matter which. Just far enough apart that you can tell." Chirp. Bedroom.

Five boxes. She was sweating a little, in socks, at 3:20 in the morning.

"You know it's because of the cold," he said. "Why they always go at night. The house cools down, the battery voltage drops, and it crosses the line around two, three. Every one of these calls in thirty-one years, it was three a.m. I used to get them from people on my route."

"People called their mailman about their smoke detectors?"

"People called their mailman about everything. I knew every door from Culver to the tracks. 14609. People think a ZIP code is a place. It's not a place. It's a list of doors, in the order you walk them." He paused. "Did you do your clocks?"

"The clocks do themselves now, Dad."

"The clocks went back last Sunday."

"I know. It's dark at five."

"It's dark at four fifty-one. And you change the batteries the day the clocks change. Fall back, new batteries. Spring forward, new batteries. That's the whole rule. It's the only day of the year everybody already remembers is a day."

She stood in her bedroom with five boxes and understood, slowly, the way you understand things at 3:20 in the morning, that last Sunday was the first fall in forty-odd years that he hadn't gone around his house on a step stool. The clocks at Linden Court were set from somewhere else. The detectors there were wired into a panel down the hall that somebody else tested. And the one detector that had been his job was in a box in her apartment, saying so once a minute.

"Three and two," he said. "Come on. You're almost there."

Three back out to the hall. Chirp. Bedroom. Two boxes left by her bed, both KITCHEN. She moved one into the hall and didn't even have to wait the full minute.

Chirp. Hall.

One box. KITCHEN (TOP DRAWER, LEFT OF STOVE). She got her keys and ran the little saw edge down the tape.

On top, wrapped in a dish towel, was the detector, still with a strip of painter's tape across its face where someone, him, had pulled it off the ceiling the day the movers came. Under it were the good flashlight, a roll of electrical tape, a tape measure, a black Sharpie with a rubber band around it, and a sealed four-pack of 9-volt batteries.

He had packed the fix with the problem. Of course he had. It was the top drawer left of the stove. Everything you needed in the first ten seconds of anything going wrong was in that drawer, and he had moved the whole drawer.

She thumbed the detector open. The old battery had *NOV 25* written on it in his capitals. Chirp, right in her hand, loud enough that she nearly dropped it.

"You found it," he said.

"There's a date on the battery."

"There's a date on every battery. How else do you know?" Then, before she could say anything else in the voice she could feel coming: "The Sharpie's in there. The one with the rubber band. Write the date on the new one before you put it in, or you'll put it in and forget and then you won't know, will you."

She put the phone on the box and wrote *NOV 26* on the new battery. Her capitals came out smaller than his and leaned the wrong way. She snapped it in and pressed the test button. The detector shrieked once, an enormous, healthy sound, and the radiator in the hall clanked as if answering.

Then nothing. She stood in the hallway among forty-one boxes and listened to a full minute of nothing.

"Is it quiet?" he said.

"It's quiet."

"Good. That's what it sounds like when it's working." The chair creaked. "Now don't leave it in the box. It can't do anything in a box. Put it on a ceiling."

"It's three-thirty."

"Then it's on the ceiling at three-thirty-five. You have a step stool?"

She did. It was in the hall, under PAPERS (?). She started moving boxes.
