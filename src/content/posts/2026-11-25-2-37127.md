The voicemail came in at 6:12 a.m., which was the first sign, because Walt Brenneman did not believe in mornings and had said so in print, in the acknowledgments of a 1997 monograph.

"Dee. Dee. Call me. No, don't call me, read it first. It's on the arXiv. No, it's on GitHub, they don't do arXiv anymore, they just put it on GitHub like a grocery list. Omega is nine fourths. Not less than. *Is.* There's a lower bound. Dee, there's a *lower bound.* Read it and call me. Don't call me before you read it."

The second voicemail came in at 6:14.

"Also Google put out a bunch of papers, discrete logs, something about sign matrices, I don't know, I haven't looked, who cares. And they're saying factoring's next! Change your bank password! Actually don't, it won't help. Call me."

By the time Dee Fennimore had found her glasses, the department group chat had a hundred and forty unread messages. The first one was a link. The second one was Raúl from the office next door, who wrote only *lol*, and then, four minutes later, *sorry Dee*, and then, two hours after that, *genuinely sorry, I was laughing about the title fight, not you.* There was a title fight. Somebody on Hacker News had written O instead of Θ, and the internet had spent the night explaining to itself the difference between a function and a problem. Four hundred and ninety comments. Someone in Lisbon had gotten very upset about epsilon.

Dee's thesis was titled *An Improved Upper Bound on the Exponent of Matrix Multiplication via Asymmetric Hashing of the Laser Method*. It improved the bound from 2.37130 to 2.37127. She had a draft of 212 pages and a defense penciled in for April. She had a notebook, an actual paper notebook, green, in which she had written the number 2.37127 on the inside cover the night she got it, in ballpoint, and drawn a little box around it, and then a second box around the first box, which in retrospect was the most honest thing in the notebook.

Five years. Three hundred-thousandths. A forty-year chase in which every few years somebody shaved another sliver off the number, like monks taking turns at a very large and very slow block of cheese. She had been the most recent monk. She had been planning to be the most recent monk for about eighteen months, which was the average.

The paper was a hundred and forty pages. She read the abstract four times and the first lemma for two hours. The first lemma was correct. She could tell it was correct the way you can tell a building is load-bearing: not by understanding it but by noticing everything else was standing on it.

At ten she called Walt back.

"Did you read it?"

"I read the first lemma."

"Isn't it *gorgeous*? I read it at the Waffle House on 27 at two in the morning and I cried into my hashbrowns, Dee, I really did, and the waitress asked me if somebody died and I said no, ma'am, somebody was *born*."

"Walt."

"Thirty-five years I've been wrong about that number. I said 2.3 in a talk in Oberwolfach in '94 and a Hungarian threw a pretzel at me. Nine fourths! It's so *clean*. You know what that means? It means the universe was being reasonable the whole time and we were just stupid. I love that. I love being stupid in a reasonable universe."

"My thesis is about 2.37127."

There was a pause on the line. She could hear, in the background, what sounded like a television, or possibly a second waitress.

"Well," Walt said, more gently, "it still is."

"It's a bound that's now off by twelve hundredths. It's a bound on a thing that's *known.* It's like publishing an upper bound on the number of legs on a dog."

"Some physicist wrote a whole thing," Walt said, "about how they're three months behind us in the stages of grief. Three months! We're *ahead*, Dee. For once in my life mathematics is ahead of physics on something." He sounded genuinely proud of this. "Come by Friday. Bring the green notebook. We'll figure out what you're for."

She did not like the phrasing. She hung up and drove to Home Depot.

## Afternoon

She did not need anything at Home Depot. She knew this the whole way there, and the knowing did not slow her down. She walked every aisle, in order, because it was the only remaining activity in her life with a well-defined procedure. In aisle 7 she held a spray bottle for a long time and thought about the one-way valve inside it, which nobody had ever written a paper about improving by three hundred-thousandths, which seemed to her that afternoon like a kind of grace. In aisle 14 she bought a pair of wraparound safety goggles, $6.97, ANSI Z87.1, with a little foam gasket.

She knew what she was going to do with them before she reached the register. She had seen the video. Everybody had seen the video. A man in Florida had put on a snorkel and gone face-down into the retention pond behind a Home Depot and found, in four feet of brown water between a highway and a loading dock, an entire world, and he had narrated it the way other men narrate cathedrals.

The pond behind her Home Depot was the size of a tennis court and fenced on one side only, as though the fence had lost interest. There were cattails, and a sign that said NO SWIMMING with a cartoon person drowning very calmly. She left her shoes and her phone on the bank. She did a quick scan for alligators, which is something you can say in Florida without irony, and waded in to her waist in her jeans.

The goggles leaked immediately, at the temples. She put her face in anyway.

Bluegill. That was the first thing: bluegill, dozens, hanging in the green light like ornaments, each one turning to look at her with the flat appraising stare of a reviewer. A shopping cart on its side, furred with algae, a crayfish living in the child seat. A gar, long as her forearm, motionless, needle-nosed, so prehistoric it looked like it had been sent by a museum to supervise. And then, unhurried, a banded water snake, which crossed her field of vision left to right with the air of someone who had a meeting.

She came up spitting. She went back down.

The thing about a retention pond is that nobody puts fish in it. It is a hole dug by a contractor to catch the runoff from a parking lot. It connects to nothing. And yet: bluegill. She remembered, floating there, a thread she had read once at 3 a.m. instead of working, a hundred strangers arguing about exactly this. Ducks, someone said, because fish eggs stick to their feet. Ducks, someone else said, but the other end: a few eggs survive the trip through the gut and come out viable in the next pond over. Floods. Storm drains. A guy with a bucket, because there is always a guy with a bucket. And somebody had proposed, with total seriousness, the experiment: dig a hundred isolated ponds, film them around the clock, and measure the mean time for nature to find a way. Nobody would ever fund it. There was no prize in it. There was no lab racing to post it on GitHub at midnight, because there was nothing to verify, nothing to check, no lemma for anything to stand on. There was just a pond, and fish in it, and the route they took to get there lost forever in the digestive tract of a mallard. And it occurred to Dee, with her face in four feet of runoff, that 2.37127 had arrived exactly the same way. Not by any route that mattered. Not because the world needed it. It had been carried in on somebody's feet, survived a long ugly passage through a gut, and dropped, viable, into a hole that happened to be there, and she had stood over it for five years and called it hers.

She had wanted it to be hers. That was the thing. Not the number. Walt had wanted the number. Walt had wanted the number so badly he cried in a Waffle House when somebody else found it. She had wanted the box around the box.

There was a line she had read somewhere that month, in some essay everybody was passing around, about how treating the process as an end in itself was profoundly self-serving. She had been offended by it at the time. She had drafted a rebuttal in her head on the drive to campus. Floating in the pond, she tried to remember the rebuttal and found that it consisted mostly of the words *but I worked really hard*, arranged in several different orders.

The gar had not moved. The gar did not care who found anything.

## Evening

When she came up the last time there was a man in an orange apron standing on the bank next to her shoes, holding her phone, which was buzzing.

"Ma'am," he said. "There's a sign."

"I saw it."

"What are you doing?"

She thought about it honestly. It was the first question anyone had asked her all day that nobody else on Earth was positioned to answer faster.

"Research," she said.

"On what?"

"I don't know yet."

He looked at the goggles. He looked at the receipt sticking out of her jeans pocket. He looked, for a long moment, at the phone, where a message from Walt was lighting up the screen: *ALSO THE RIEMANN PEOPLE ARE HAVING A PARTY IN SPAIN, DO YOU WANT TO GO, I'LL PAY*.

"Those are from aisle 14," he said.

"They leak."

"They're not for that."

"I know," Dee said, and put her face back in the water, and started counting bluegill, and lost count at nineteen when the snake came back, and started over, badly, from one.
