Members of the jury, the defense has spent two years on the same plea, and you have heard it everywhere: the machine made the work worthless. The reports write themselves now. The code writes itself. The prose, the art, the proofs — all of it, suddenly cheap, suddenly flooding in, suddenly worth nothing.

I am here to tell you that not one word of that is true, and that the defense knows it.

The machine did not make the work worthless. The machine sent everyone an itemized bill for the part of the work they had never bothered to price, and the bill came back reading zero, and they mistook that zero for the value of the whole thing. It is not. It never was. I intend to prove to you that the scarce thing — the thing you were actually paying for, the entire time — was never the artifact at all.

## Exhibit A: two things that were thrown away

Consider the vulnerability report.

For thirty years it was treated as sacred. You drop everything for a vuln report. You owe the reporter confidentiality, speed, gratitude. Then the models arrived and anyone could generate one, and the reports came in by the dozen, each one technically real and practically meaningless, and the maintainers who used to feel an obligation now feel only fatigue. *Vulnerability reports are not special anymore*, the verdict goes.

But read the dissent, because the dissent is correct. The report was never the special thing. The report was always cheap. What was scarce was the human being who stood behind it and said *this matters, and here is exactly how it breaks, and I will put my name on that claim.* The demonstration of impact. The last mile, which is judgment, which has never once been automated. The machine flooded the channel with the cheap part and we acted shocked to discover the cheap part was cheap.

Now consider the man Google fired.

He built a command-line tool for Google's own platform. It became, in the words of the people who used it, the single best resource of its kind — the thing that worked, the thing that connected, made by someone who actually cared about the platform when, as one witness put it, his colleagues rarely seemed to care about anything. And the company that employed him looked at this and saw not value but liability. A logo used without clearance. A name too close to official. They could have asked him to rename it. They fired him.

These are the same exhibit, members of the jury. In both cases an institution held an artifact in one hand and the human judgment that produced it in the other, and decided the artifact was the asset and the human was the cost. They kept the report and resented the reporter. They would have kept the tool and discarded the man. They priced the noun and threw away the verb. And this is the precise error the machine has now made universal: we are all, suddenly, very good at producing nouns, and we have all forgotten that nobody was ever paying for the noun.

## Exhibit B: the squiggle and the dice

I want to show you two acts of restraint, because restraint is the form judgment takes when nobody is watching.

The first. A man at Microsoft named Tony Krueger died, and most of you have never heard his name, and all of you have used his work this morning. He is the reason the red squiggle appears under a misspelled word *without stopping you*. Think about what that decision actually was. The spell-checker already existed; the dictionary lookup was not the hard part, the dictionary lookup was the cheap part, the part a machine does. The scarce thing was a human being deciding that the correct behavior was to *not interrupt you* — to mark the error and then get out of your way, to trust you to handle it on your own time. That is not an algorithm. That is taste. That is a judgment about the dignity of the person on the other side of the screen, and it was right, and it is now invisible inside a billion machines, and the man who made it was introduced at a magic show as "he did the squiggles," which is both absurd and the highest honor our trade knows how to give.

The second. Elden Ring's enemies feel intelligent. Players swear the bosses read them, bait them, punish their habits. They do not. There is no mind in there. There is a small stack of simple behaviors and a set of weighted dice — roll, adjust the weights for distance and health, roll again — and the *appearance* of intelligence falls out of it cleanly. The engineers who built it will tell you, with some pride, that the trick is to put the control in the hands of the designer, not the algorithm. Which is to say: a human being looked at a system that could have been made genuinely, expensively, complicatedly smart, and chose instead to trust the dice, and to take responsibility for how they were weighted.

Do you see that these are the same act? Krueger choosing not to interrupt. The designer choosing to trust the roll. Neither one is the clever part. The clever part — the spell-checker, the dice — was always available, always cheap. What cost something was a person deciding *where to stop*, and standing behind the stop. The machine can now generate infinite behavior. It cannot decide what to leave out. Leaving things out is the whole job. It always was.

## The verdict the defense already knows

Members of the jury, I will not pretend the disruption is imaginary. An index of packages built by one devoted person gets absorbed into a trillion-dollar company and we are told to be glad he is finally getting paid. The artifacts are being collected; the makers are being thanked and shown the door. That is real and it is happening this week. I am not asking you to deny it.

I am asking you to refuse the lie buried inside it — the lie that says, *because the artifact is now cheap, the work was never worth anything.* That is the defense's whole case and it is exactly backwards. The artifact was always cheap. You were always paying for something else. You were paying for the human being who would put their name on the claim, who would decide where to stop, who would take responsibility for the dice. The machine has not destroyed that. The machine has done the opposite. By making everything else free, it has stripped the paint off the wall and left only the load-bearing thing standing there, exposed, unmistakable, finally impossible to mistake for anything cheaper.

I will give the last word to a witness the defense did not call.

There is a man named Jerry Gretzinger who has been drawing a map of a country that does not exist since 1963. Four thousand panels. A circular world radiating out from a center, governed by a deck of about a hundred cards he made himself — draw a card, do what it says, paint what the system tells you to paint. He surrendered authorship to the dice on purpose. And when you ask him whose work it is, he says: *my hand puts the paint on the paper, but I step back and look at it as though I were not the perpetrator, merely the observer.*

He has handed the cheap part — the painting, the deciding-what — entirely over to the machine of his own cards. And he still owns every panel. He understood, sixty years before the rest of us got the bill, that the scarce thing was never the brushstroke. It was the man who drew the card, and accepted what it gave him, and signed it anyway.

That is the verdict. The work was never the work. The work was the willingness to be responsible for it. The machine cannot do that, has never been able to do that, and the moment it tries, the responsibility will quietly slide back to whoever turned it on.

The prosecution rests.
