**ATLAS OF WARS — Entry 4,412.** *The Scraper Wars.* Theater: the open web. Duration: ongoing, though the outcome was decided early. Combatants: everyone. Front line: none — which is the first thing the map cannot show you, and the most important.

The trouble with charting this war is that there are no borders and no uniforms. The attacking force does not arrive; it *resolves*, out of a hundred thousand home internet connections that belong to people who have no idea they've been conscripted. A residential proxy is a bot wearing your neighbor's face — routed through a cheap streaming box, a compromised phone, a smart fridge that agreed to something in paragraph forty of a terms-of-service no human read. The crawler that strip-mines a small forum for training data now knocks on the door looking exactly like a retiree in Ohio checking the weather. You cannot ban the enemy, because the enemy is indistinguishable from your own population. This is the condition every collapsing network eventually reaches: the point at which you can no longer tell a trading partner from a raider, because they are arriving in the same ship.

They have arrived in the same ship before.

## The last time the routes turned predatory

Around 1177 BC the Late Bronze Age did not so much end as *unthread*. It was the first true globalized economy — Egypt, the Hittites, Mycenae, Ugarit, Babylon, all locked into a single web of trade because bronze itself demanded it: copper from one shore, tin from a thousand miles away, the metal that armed and fed everyone impossible to make alone. It was efficient. It was specialized. It was, in the language of our own industry, *tightly coupled*. And tight coupling is a wonderful property right up to the moment stress arrives, at which point it becomes the mechanism of your death. Drought, raiders, a few broken routes, and the palaces went dark one after another like servers losing a shared dependency. Nobody conquered the Bronze Age. It was a distributed system, and it failed the way distributed systems fail: not at a point, but everywhere, quickly, because everything was load-bearing for everything else.

We built the same machine and admired it for the same reasons. Consider that this month researchers disclosed a flaw — call it by its name, GhostLock — that sat inside *every* Linux distribution on earth for fifteen years, introduced in one kernel version and quietly load-bearing beneath the entire edifice ever since. One recipe for bronze; one kernel under the world. Monoculture is just efficiency that has forgotten it is also risk. The Hittites kept three years of grain and still starved when the fourth dry year came. We keep one code path and call it a standard.

## Doctrine: the salting of one's own fields

Here is where the atlas must correct the popular history, which casts this as a war of invaders against defenders, machines against the human web. It is not. The decisive doctrine of the Scraper Wars was developed by the defenders, and it is *arson*.

There is now a growing, earnest community whose strategy is to poison the wells — to pump hundreds of gigabytes of deliberate garbage per day into the crawlers: fabricated text, corrupted data, plausible nonsense seeded into repositories and pages precisely so that anything harvested is worthless. They are proud of it. The poison, they'll tell you, gets better every day. And it might. But notice what it is. It is not a wall around the commons. It is a decision that the commons should be made undrinkable so the enemy cannot drink — which necessarily means *you* cannot drink either. You do not salt a field you expect to farm again. You salt it on the way out, when you've already conceded the valley, when denying the harvest to the raider matters more to you than the harvest. The scorched granary is not a sign of a defense holding. It is the tell of a people who have privately agreed the war is lost and are now settling accounts with the ground.

This is the grimly funny part, if you have the historian's distance for it: both armies are now largely fighting phantoms. The scrapers wear their victims' faces; the defenders feed poison to machines that shrug it off and keep eating; and somewhere in the middle a forum moderator bans a zealot mid-thread for being too single-minded about the whole affair. Everyone is winning. The valley is on fire.

## The looting, and what the fire clears

Collapse has a reliable etiquette: while the walls come down, the elites strip the temple. So it is unsurprising, and barely worth a line in the atlas except for completeness, that the season's marquee lawsuit has one giant accusing another of walking out the door with the trade secrets in its pockets — Apple against OpenAI, palace against palace, each certain the other is the barbarian. This is not the collapse. This is the ornament on it, the squabble over the good silver as the roof goes.

The collapse's actual work is clearing ground. Every commons that fails becomes real estate, and the enclosers are already surveying. The open sky is next: the plan on the table is to add a hundred thousand more satellites to low orbit, a private mesh strung across the last shared ceiling, sold — plausibly, even generously — as connection for everyone. That is how enclosure always sells itself. The Bronze Age routes were open until they weren't; then a few held the straits and charged toll. A commons doesn't die into emptiness. It dies into ownership.

## Assessment

The healthiest thing a shared system can be is *invisible* — a good tool you never think about, the way you never think about the road until it's out. For thirty years the open web was exactly that: infrastructure nobody stared at because it simply worked. The surest measure of how far this war has already gone is that nobody can stop staring at it now. We talk about the plumbing constantly, obsessively, because the plumbing is what's on fire.

Outcome: undetermined, in the sense that the last palace has not yet gone dark. Determined, in every sense that matters, because the people inside have stopped defending the walls and started poisoning the wells. Historians will note that no one surrendered. They will note that there was no one to surrender *to*. They will note the date this entry was opened, and leave the closing date blank, and move on to Entry 4,413 — some older, cleaner war, the kind with a winner.
