The text this morning is two numbers. Eight percent, and sixty percent.

They are the before and after of juvenile coho salmon survival on French Creek, in the Scott River watershed in northern California. Here is what happened in between. People hammered wooden posts into the streambed. They wove willow and conifer branches through the posts. They plugged the gaps with gravel and straw and mud. That is the whole technology. Nine thousand square meters of new habitat, water that stayed cool enough that a young fish did not cook in it, and salmon coming back to that river through a drought that emptied others.

And then the beavers moved in and redid the work, and one of the people who built it said theirs was far superior to what we can accomplish.

Now understand what that structure is, because everything else I am going to say depends on it. A beaver dam leaks. It leaks by design, all day and all night. It does not stop the water, it slows the water and pushes it sideways into the cold pools where the fish live. It is also falling apart at every moment, and it stands only because something goes out in the dark and rebuilds it. Every night. Forever.

So here is the claim, and I am not going to soften it: permanence is not a property you can build into a thing. It is a bill. It arrives every night, and everything you currently believe is permanent is simply something whose bill someone else has been quietly paying without telling you.

Neil Fraser has been paying his for twenty-five years. He registered neil.fraser.name a quarter century ago, and registered one for his daughter minutes after she was born, and he has paid the invoice through the year 2040. In February 2027 the domain will stop existing, because Verisign asked ICANN for permission to simplify their administration and got it, and twenty-two thousand people learned about it from their registrars afterward. In the same breath, and it is the same breath: on the Fairphone thread, where everyone is admiring a phone built to be repaired forever, somebody points out that you cannot get Fairphone 3 parts anymore, they ran dry in 2024, and somebody else says this reminds him of the throwing axe he bought specifically because it advertised replaceable handles, and nobody anywhere sells the handles. The prepaid domain and the axe handle are the same object. Both men bought a future from a party under no obligation to keep it in stock.

In November of last year the sun threw several large pieces of itself at us, the ionosphere lit up, and positions across the United States slid by as much as thirty three feet. The fixed frame moved. I forgive this entirely. The sun does not owe you a reference, and the fix is beautiful: you do not recover accuracy by building a more rigid frame, you recover it with a second receiver on a known point that is being wrong in the same direction at the same instant, and you subtract.

Now the other one. A company whose entire product was custody of the one fact about you that cannot be rotated, your face on a government document, had a live feed running out the back of it for more than a year. Not a breach. A feed. You cannot reissue your face, you cannot pick a new date of birth, and the reason this is not the sun's kind of accident is that nobody chose the solar storm and somebody chose the budget. Somebody sat in a room and decided what the logging would cost and who would be watching it, and the answer to the second question turned out to be strangers, for a year, in real time.

Meanwhile Ted Nelson spent sixty years designing a link that could not break and shipped none of them, and Google shipped a working one to millions of people and switches it off in January. Transclusion, two way links, the permascroll, the whole cathedral of never rotting, against a checkbox that lets a florist send mail from her own domain. One was engineered to be eternal and never existed. The other existed and dies on a Tuesday. Score it however you like.

Look instead at what is actually still standing, and notice that nobody is bragging about the structure. An Elevator of the Year award, an award that exists, and thank God for it, went to a modernization. The Metropolis Trust Building, a traction system from around 1907, still carrying people in 2026, still there for exactly one reason: it has been torn out and put back roughly once a generation for a hundred and nineteen years. Somebody on the thread looked at a photograph of a brake assembly bolted to a skid, bolted to I beams, bolted to another skid, bolted to the floor, and captioned it, this is just temporary, said the construction worker in 1907. He was right. It was temporary. That is why it is still running. Somebody else's 1993 Amiga game is playable again this month because he sat down and read its 68000 assembly line by line and carried it into Godot, which is a nightly bill paid thirty three years late, in one sitting, by one man who wanted his pond back.

And take the counterexample seriously, because I am not here to pick only winners. Shin Jinseo beat KataGo at a two stone handicap. Two stones is roughly the distance between the strongest professional alive and a rookie professional, and the machine spotted him that and lost. Not because it degraded. Because it never stopped being exactly what it was. It learned from even games between equals, so it has no repertoire for a board where the correct play is to hand a stronger opponent room to blunder. It kept choosing the highest probability move in a world it was never shown. That is what a finished structure looks like when the water changes course. Perfectly built, perfectly still, and the river going around it.

So this is what I want from you.

Stop asking whether a thing is permanent. Ask who is paying for it tonight, and what happens on the first night they stop, and if you cannot name that person then the thing is not permanent, it is only recent. Ask it about your domain, your mail, your identity provider, your dependencies, the format your life's work is sitting in.

And then stop building monuments and start building invitations. The people in the Scott River watershed did not build a dam. They put posts in a creek and wove willow between them and hoped, and the dam was built afterward by animals who showed up and did it better. That is the entire trick and it is available to every one of you. Put in the posts. Publish the format. Keep the parts in stock. Leave the assembly readable. Sell the handle for the axe. Then get out of the way, and when it leaks, do not seal it, because the leak is where the cold water goes.

Eight percent to sixty. Nobody ever got that out of concrete.

Go hammer in a post.
