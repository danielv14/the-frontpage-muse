Sit down. No, here. This chair. I know it wobbles.

You were going to leave it and come back at two. Everybody was going to leave it and come back at two. We don't take drop-offs. It's rule one on the laminated sheet and it's the only rule anybody argues about, which tells you something about which rule is doing the work.

It isn't that I want the company. It's that in about nine minutes you are going to see the inside of your own lamp, and after that you will never again be a person who has not seen the inside of a lamp. That's what you came for. The switch is free.

Pass me the small Phillips. No, the other small one.

Behind you, Marek is putting a fourth patch into a pair of jeans that are at this point mostly patch, and the woman by the window has been truing a bike wheel for an hour, badly, with enormous satisfaction. Somebody came in at ten with three kitchen knives she was about to throw out. Throw out. Knives. Nobody had ever told her they could be sharpened. That is the single most common thing that happens in this building: not stupidity, just nobody ever told her. We handed her the stone and she did it herself and came back twenty minutes later to show me a tomato.

Rule two is clean your item before you bring it in, and rule two exists because an apprentice of mine opened a vacuum in 2023 and live bed bugs walked out of it.

Right. First screw. Hold that.

Here's what I've learned doing this three years. Almost nothing is hidden. It's closed. Those are completely different things and people mix them up constantly.

Somebody spent last week pulling apart a Quake CD from 1996, one of those shareware discs where you rang an 800 number to unlock the rest of the games, and the whole scheme fell over because the unlock program on the disc could work out the answer by itself. It never needed the phone call. There was even a plain text file sitting next to the encrypted one saying more or less the same thing. Thirty-nine days before someone cracked it, and the only reason it took thirty-nine is that nobody had bothered to look for the first thirty-eight. And then this week a fellow writes up how a certain app hides its logo underneath a button so the logo shows up in your screenshots, explains the mechanism completely, and two hundred and fifty people pile in underneath to argue about it, and five of them separately have to ask the others whether they read the article. It was open. It was open the entire time. Nobody opened it.

There's a whole row going on somewhere about people posting text they didn't write to people who won't read it, which is a closed loop, and I wish them all well. Somebody went to the bother of inventing an entire research institute, staff and papers and all, purely so the machines would have something to quote. A librarian has put out a guide to switching every bit of that off in your own software. It starts: put on your own oxygen mask first. Hold the shade steady.

Second screw. There's always one that's been overtightened by a previous man with a big screwdriver and a theory.

There's a second-hand bookshop in Wellington getting orders it can't explain. Obscure titles, in bulk, the sort of thing that sells twice a decade. The fast way to scan a book is to slice the spine off and feed the loose pages through, so somewhere those books stop being books and become a number, and the number is worth more to somebody than the book was. Meanwhile there is a person I will never meet who has got the main camera working on a phone whose manufacturer had already finished with it. Autofocus, colour correction, given away. The pictures are still grainy and they say so in the post, which I liked. And they were over the moon about their first donation.

Same question underneath both of those. Who gets to say a thing is done. It's almost never the person holding it.

Now. See that tablet at the end of the bench, the one where the screen is lifting off the case like a lid coming up on a pan.

Battery. Swollen. Completely normal, lithium does that, nobody did anything wrong. The battery is twelve pounds and I have three in the drawer.

I can get into it. Heat mat, plastic pick, twenty minutes and patience. Getting in is not the problem.

The screen is the structure. No screws anywhere in it, the glass is what holds the case square, and there is adhesive round the entire perimeter, the good kind, the kind that means it. So I can open it, and I can fit the battery, and then I have a working tablet in two pieces and a roll of double-sided tape that is not the tape they used, because the tape they used is not sold. And the new cell has a little chip on it that has to introduce itself to the mainboard, and it won't, because it isn't theirs.

I can open it. I can't close it. Those are two problems and only one of them is mine.

I want to be precise here, because people expect me to go soft at this bit and I am not going soft. Nothing broke. That's the part. Every component in that tablet works. It is a functioning machine that was ruled finished six years ago by somebody in a meeting who chose glue over four screws, and at two o'clock a man is coming back for it and I have to tell him that while the thing sits there working.

Four screws. That is the entire difference. Not a technology, not the market, not the century. Four screws, and a room full of people who decided against them.

And no, don't start. Someone's got a piece up about not enjoying the internet any more and I am sure it is beautifully written. Things broke in 1994. My father's television broke constantly and a man came to the house about it. The difference was never that things break. The difference is who is allowed to look.

Right. There's your fault. See it, that copper tag, gone black and lifted off the contact. Heat, over years, from a bulb that was too hot for the fitting. That's the whole thing. Two minutes with the file.

No, I'm not doing that bit. You are. Take the screwdriver.

Because this was never about the lamp. Anybody can fix a lamp, the lamp took nine minutes and cost nothing, and if all you wanted was light there's a shop on the corner. What you're taking home is that you have now been inside one thing. One. And the next time something in your house stops working, before you get your coat, you are going to have a thought you did not have this morning, which is: I wonder what it looks like in there.

That thought is the whole movement. The rest is a folding chair and a box of screws.

Two o'clock. Next.
