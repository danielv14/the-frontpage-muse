Thomas Austin turned John Lewis off at a quarter past one on a Tuesday, beside the pug mill, with the horse for a witness.

He did it the way he did everything, which was loudly and in round numbers. John was done. John could have his wages Saturday. John could take his cleverness to Burslem, where they had more use for it, or to the devil, who had the most. The horse went on walking its circle, because nobody had told it to stop, and the blades in the mill went on cutting the marl, and the noise of it all covered nothing, so that every man at Clay Hills heard.

The cleverness in question had happened that morning. A gentleman's man had come up from Hanley about tiles for a new chapel, and Austin had named him a price by the thousand, and the gentleman's man had written it in a little book and turned the book round for Austin to make his mark. John, carrying clay past, had read the figure upside down and said, in a helpful voice, that the master had said fourteen shillings and the book said four. He had been right. The gentleman's man had been very sorry. Austin had made his mark, a cross with a hook on one arm, and had not looked at John again until a quarter past one.

So. Saturday.

There was the rest of the afternoon to get through, and John was a moulder, and the clay was tempered and waiting, and a man does not stop being good at a thing because another man has decided to stop paying him for it. He went back to his bench.

The bench was a plank on two trestles under a lean-to. On it were the mould, a frame of beech shod with iron at the corners and no bigger than a hymn book; a tub of sand; a bowl of water; the strike, a flat stick worn to the shape of his own palm; and the nibbing stick for pinching up the two little hooks that hang a tile on its batten. That was the whole of the trade. Everything else was in the hands. You took a clot of the marl, heavier than it looked, and you threw it hard into the sanded mould so that it filled the corners by itself, because clay that has been pushed remembers being pushed and warps in the kiln. You struck off the top in one pass. You pinched the nibs. You lifted the mould away and the tile stood on its pallet, square and grey and wet, and the off-bearer boy carried it to the hacks to dry. Throw, strike, nib, lift. A fair moulder made his thousand in a day. John made more, and his came out of the kiln flat, and rang when you tapped them, and nobody at Clay Hills had ever mentioned it, because you do not mention the weather.

He made forty-odd tiles before the thought came to him, and three more while he decided.

The boy, Sam, came back for the next one and found John holding a nail.

It is a strange thing to write in clay. Paper takes ink and is finished with it. Clay takes a line and gives back a little lip on either side, so the letter has a floor and walls, and you feel the nail go down rather than across. John had learned his letters at the Sunday school in Shelton from a Methodist who smelled of peppermints, and he had never written anything longer than his own name or a list of what was owed him. He held the nail like a quill, which was wrong, and then like a chisel, which was better.

The question was what to say. He thought about it the way he would think about a tile, which is to say he thought about what had to bear the weight. Austin had said a great deal by the pug mill, and almost none of it would bear anything. Burslem would not keep. The devil would not keep. Under all that noise there had been one plain sentence, and John knew how it went, because he had stood in a potbank once while the steward read one out to a man and the man had stood there with his cap in his hands while it was read to him, as if the reading made it more true than the saying.

*I do hereby discharge you, John Lewis, from my employment,* he wrote, on the back of the tile.

His hand was better than he had expected. The letters leaned together a little, like men walking home.

Then he came to the name, and stopped, because this was the part that mattered.

Thomas Austin had never written his own name in his life. It had been written for him all the same, a great many times: on the carter's tickets, in the gentleman's man's little book, at the foot of the lease on the yard where the attorney had put *Thomas Austin, his mark* and Austin had added his hooked cross beside it. The name was a thing other people set down about him. Now John was going to set it down, and John wanted to be clear in his own mind about what he was doing.

Were they Austin's words? Austin had said them, or something louder that meant them. Austin had certainly meant them. But Austin could not have put them here, or anywhere that lasted. What Austin said by the pug mill had gone up with the horse's breath and would be forgotten by every man in the yard before Christmas, the way a man's temper ought to be forgotten. That was the mercy in talk: it blew away. A master could say anything in his own yard, in front of his own men, and trust it to blow away. Now one sentence of it was not going to. John had taken what was said in heat, among friends and enemies, and cut it down to the part that would bear weight and given it a spelling, and when the kiln was done with it, it would be said forever, to anybody, in Thomas Austin's name.

So whose was it? The clay was the field's. The sentence was the master's. The hand was John's, and the choosing. He turned it over and could not make it come out to one man, and he found he did not mind. The tile would say Thomas Austin discharged John Lewis, and that would be perfectly true, and the only proof of it in the whole world would be in the handwriting of the man discharged.

*Thomas Austin,* he wrote, and made the T handsome, with a little tail on the crossbar. If a man was going to be remembered for one sentence, it seemed only fair that the sentence should be properly spelt and the name decently signed. He gave the master a better signature than the master had ever had.

"What's it say?" said Sam, who could not read.

"Says I'm discharged."

Sam looked at the tile and then at John. "Who wrote it?"

"Master did," said John, which was as near the truth as he could get it, and near enough to go on a roof.

Then there was the matter of what to do with it. He could take the strike to it in one pass and nobody the wiser. He could lay it in the hack face up, where Austin would walk past it every morning and not be able to read a word, and there was a pleasure in that, but not enough of one. Or he could let it go with the rest. Austin's dog had run across a row of green tiles the week before, and nobody had troubled to scrape the prints off, and those had gone into the kiln and would go up on a roof somewhere, paws and all, because a tile is a tile. A roof tile hangs by its nibs with its back to the inside of the roof, under the course above, in the dark. Nobody on the ground would ever see this one. Nobody in the loft would either, unless they went up with a candle on purpose. It would hang there as long as the roof stood, and if John's tiles were any judge, that would be a long time. He understood, holding it, that he was writing for someone who did not exist yet. That would be whoever came when the chapel or the barn finally came down, long after Austin was dead and John was dead and Clay Hills was a field again, and turned a tile over in their hands and found a sentence. He found he had written more carefully for that person than he had ever written for anyone living. You do, for a reader you will never be able to explain things to afterward. You get it right the first time. You make sure it can be read in whatever light they have.

He gave it to Sam, and Sam carried it to the hacks and laid it in its row with the others, and John went back to his bench and made tiles until the light went, because it was still Tuesday and he was still a moulder.

The burner set the kiln the week after and gave it a long slow fire, damped down at the end so the iron in the marl went blue, and the tile came out flat and blue and rang when you tapped it. It would be more than fifty years, and on the far side of the Atlantic, before anyone thought to use the kiln's word for what had been done to the man who made it.
