Mind the step. The lease office keeps that one cracked on purpose. Adds character. We can fix it, of course, but then a tenant would expect everything else to work too, and we are not in the business of setting expectations we can meet.

Let me walk you down the row. You said you wanted to see what's available. Everything is available. That's the whole street. Forty units, lit, swept, climate-controlled, and not one of them rented in eighteen months. People hear "vacant" and think "cheap." That's the amateur read. Vacant is the most expensive thing a building can be.

Here's the first one. Lovely glass. The sign says STOP USING JWTS, all caps, very confident, the kind of confidence that doesn't take a deposit. Asking price hasn't moved since 2019. Notice it hasn't moved since 2019 — that's not staleness, that's *conviction*. The owner published a single page declaring the entire approach unsafe, full stop, and then went to lunch. People come by. People always come by. They press their faces to the glass and they say, all right, if it's so unsafe, go hack the front door — go hack the actual production lock that ships behind this exact mechanism, the one running the model everybody talks to, and come back with the keys. And the owner says the standard is simply not trusted by experts. And the tenant says by which experts, and the owner says go read the other page, which links to a third page, which is from 2017 and is mostly annoyed. And the tenant takes their face off the glass and walks two doors down.

Two doors down is the big one. You'll like this. Floor-to-ceiling. The marquee reads — let me get it right — "we are transforming the operating system into an *intelligence system*, one that anticipates user needs and creates more opportunities for engagement with your apps." Same building, really, as the JWT one. I don't separate them on the tour. Both are an asking price the street already declined to pay, dressed up so the assessor won't notice nobody's inside.

See, they raised the rent on that building by bundling in a feature. A movie promo. Shipped it inside a *security update* — the lock got more expensive because they glued a billboard to it. And here is the part the brochure leaves out: watch the foot traffic. Watch where it goes. It goes across the street, to the unmarked door, the one a volunteer cut into the wall so people could strip the billboard back off their own phone. That's not vandalism. That's a tenant leaving. The heckler at the JWT glass and the man quietly carving an exit out of his Pixel are doing the identical thing in two registers — they are refusing to rent at the asking price — and my whole job, the entire job, is to narrate that exodus as if it were a velvet rope. *Exclusive. Limited access. You have to know someone to get out.*

You're looking at the empty lots between the buildings. Good eye. Don't be fooled by empty — empty is where the actual living happens. That's a man over there reconstructing HTTP by hand out of a raw socket because somebody told him the container was too small for tools. That's the local-model crowd, who decided this year that the thing finally runs on hardware they own and they don't have to rent the cloud anymore. That's a woman three lots down shaving a yak with visible joy, having gone to fetch one small thing and come back, days later, having built four. None of them are on the lease. None of them pay rent. They are squatting in the space the asking price emptied out, and they are the only people on this street getting any value out of it, and I am contractually required to walk you past them quickly.

Now. You keep glancing at the corner. I noticed at the lease office, you keep doing it. There's a building on the corner and it isn't on the tour.

Let's keep moving. Here's a storefront where one-point-three million people signed a petition to be allowed to keep the games they already bought, and the verdict came back that this would be a non-binding conversation about a voluntary code of conduct by year's end. Beautiful crown molding. The organizers are calling it a strategic relocation to a different venue, a battle lost that never really mattered. Which is the correct posture, on this street. You never lower the price. You announce that the price was always pointed at a different building.

The corner. Fine. The corner.

That one's not for lease and it never will be. The man who owned it ran the most successful shop the row ever saw — two thousand four hundred windows, lines out the door — and when the syndicate came to him with the dump truck of merchandise money, the dolls, the t-shirts, the animated tie-ins, he said no. For six years he said no. He said the moment you make the tiger a product you've killed the question of whether the tiger is real, and the question was the whole thing, the question was the entire value, and he would rather have the question than the truck. And then it exhausted him and he turned off the lights and walked away and never came back, and would not so much as wave at the place again.

And here's what I'm not supposed to tell you on the walkthrough: his work is the only thing on this entire street that's worth more now than the day it closed. Everyone else's brand went cheap. Went tacky. He's the only owner who looked at a price the market was begging to pay and said *that number is a lie about what this is* — and the proof he was right is that the corner is still dark and the corner is still priceless and you cannot have it. He didn't extend and pretend. He just left the rent honest and ate the loss with his whole body.

So that's the street. Forty units, lit, swept, empty. The vacancy is not a bug we're working on. The vacancy *is the product* — every dark window is a building telling you the truth about its price by refusing to charge it, and the entire industry up and down this row has agreed to read that confession as prestige.

You want my honest recommendation? Take the corner. I know it's not for lease. That's the point. Everything for lease here is overpriced by exactly the amount it's pretending not to be empty. The one square of the street that priced itself honestly is the one square you'll never get to rent.

Mind the step on the way out. We really should fix that.
