The tape goes around at the navel, standing, at the end of an ordinary breath out. Where exactly, and how relaxed, and how long after eating: all of it is specified somewhere in law, which is the only reason the number means anything to anybody. At seven forty that morning Akenbara Sumiko had held her arms out from her sides while a nurse read seventy-nine centimeters off the tape and entered it into a national database that would outlive her by a comfortable margin. By ten past nine she was at Window 4 of the ward office, watching a very young man try to delete her.

Not on purpose. In fifty-eight years nobody had ever done it on purpose.

Window 4 had an anti-glare film over the queue display, two grids laid down slightly out of true, so that a dark band crawled across the numbers whenever she moved her head. Sit square to it and the band went away. She sat square to it and set her folder on the counter.

"Akenbara," said the young man. His badge said MORI. "Ah. Akenbara-san. There's, um."

"There's a box," she said.

There was a box. She could see it reflected in his glasses, a soft grey suggestion panel that had opened itself over the record, and she knew without reading it what it said, because she had watched a version of that box open in 1996 and 2003 and 2011 and 2018, each time politer than the last.

*Non-standard character detected in name field. Did you mean 山女原?*

"It's asking me to accept a correction," Mori said.

"Don't."

"No. No, of course." He did not touch anything. He was, she noted with approval, one of the ones who did not touch anything. "It's just that it won't let me save the record with the field in this state. It says the field is invalid."

"It always says that."

She opened the folder. It was a cheap clear-pocket folder, the kind sold in packs of five, and it had been repacked so many times that the plastic had gone milky at the fold. Sheet one was laminated. She had not laminated it out of sentiment. She had laminated it because in 2003 a man at a different window had spilled tea on the original and she had spent four months getting another copy.

Mori was reading his own screen. He had found a summary, the way they always find a summary. "It says here the character comes from a misprint. In a Qing dynasty dictionary."

"No."

"That's what it says."

She turned the laminated page around and slid it across the counter with two fingers, the way you would slide a photograph.

It was a photocopy of a photocopy of a page from the 1997 review, and the interesting part was the picture. Two pieces of paper. The top half of one character, the bottom half of another, cut out with scissors and laid down together on the glass of a copier by somebody in 1978 who needed to record the name of a hamlet in Shiga Prefecture and had no type for it. The copier caught the join. The join came out as a horizontal stroke. Somebody further down the line looked at the copy, counted the strokes, and faithfully entered what was in front of him. It went into the national character standard that year. It went into the international standard later, out of the national one, because that is how the international one was built.

Mori looked at the picture for a while.

"So it's a mistake," he said.

"Yes."

"But they found that out. In ninety-seven. They knew."

"Yes."

He turned the page over, hoping for the part where somebody fixes it. The back of the page has the answer and it is four lines long and it is not satisfying: nothing that has been assigned is ever unassigned. Not this, not the eleven others like it, not the reserved corners of the standard that were deliberately left empty for things nobody had thought of yet. Every serious standard in the world runs on the same promise in two directions, room held open at one end and nothing taken back at the other, and if you break the second half to tidy up a smudge from 1978, then every document anyone ever wrote is only correct until the next committee has an opinion.

Mori was twenty-four and had been shown, in the space of ninety seconds, a smudge on a photocopy and the reason it is permanent, and he did the thing she had been waiting for.

"Sorry, but," he said. "That's insane."

"Isn't it wonderful," said Akenbara Sumiko.

The reading field would not accept a blank. It never does. She watched him tab into it and watched the box come back, pleased with itself, and this time it did not ask, it just filled the space: a reading, in clean kana, with a short explanatory note underneath about the character's meaning and a helpfully constructed example sentence.

"It says the reading is *sho*," Mori said. "And that it means, ah, a woman of the mountains."

"There is no reading. That's the point of it. It isn't a word."

"Then why did it say that?"

"Because you asked."

He sat back an inch. Somewhere behind his face a thing was rearranging itself, and she left it alone and let him get there, because the ones who get there on their own stay gotten there. The machine had been asked a question with no answer in it, and it did not have the word for that, because nobody writes down the sentence *and then we never found out*. It had produced three warm confident sentences instead, and it would have produced three more if he had asked again, and none of them would have been marked in any way as different from the true ones.

"Type it in the surname field by hand," she said. "From the code. It's on the second sheet."

The second sheet was the code, printed large, because in 2011 a migration had eaten her address history and she had learned to carry things printed large. Everything before she was forty-three: gone, silently, no error, the field simply shorter afterward than it had been before. She mentioned this to Mori the way you would mention a lost umbrella. It did not come back and she had stopped wanting it to.

He typed it in by hand. The record saved.

Then he printed her residence certificate, and it came off the roller warm, and there it was, first character of her surname, wrong for forty-eight years and correct on the page. He set the office seal on it in vermilion, and squared it, and passed it under the glass.

"Two, please," she said.

He printed a second. She slid one behind the laminated sheet and closed the folder over it, and that was the whole victory, an ordinary piece of A4 with an ordinary stamp on it, filed where she could find it in seven years when they replaced the system again.

She stood up. Then she sat back down, because she had one more thing and she had been saving it.

"There's another one," she said. "Of the twelve. They solved eleven. They found the hamlet, or the smudge, or the man who miscounted. One of them, they never found anything at all. Nobody knows where it came from, nobody knows what it means, nobody knows how to say it." She wrote it on the back of his notepad and turned it round. 彁. "That's in your phone. Right now. In your pocket."

Mori typed it into his screen. It rendered instantly, perfectly, at every size, the way it renders on every machine on earth. Then he asked the assistant what it meant, and the assistant told him at once, warmly, in three sentences, with an example sentence.

She was at the door when she heard it, half a laugh, the sound a person makes the first time they decide to stop believing something. She went out into the heat very pleased with her morning. Seven years was not so long, and she was already a little bit looking forward to it.
