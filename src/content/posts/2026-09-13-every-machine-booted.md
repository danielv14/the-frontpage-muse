The first one takes him forty minutes, and thirty-five of those are thermal paste.

The badge reader logs him into the building at 0217. It records that a badge was presented, that the badge is valid, and that the badge belongs to him. He is the midnight supervisor. There is no field on the reader for why.

The back office behind secondary has six workstations in it, three years old and better than they need to be. Procurement bought one configuration for the whole region and the region includes places that do real work. Here the machines run lookups. An officer types a name into a query window and waits, and outside there is a car idling with its window down and somebody's hands on the wheel at ten and two.

He has brought a Phillips number one, a tube of paste he is not going to use, and a processor he bought for thirty-one dollars.

The case comes off. The cooler comes off. The good chip lifts out of the socket with the small dry sound they make and goes into an antistatic bag inside his jacket, and the thirty-one dollar chip goes down into the socket, and the lever comes down over it. Then he scrapes the old paste off the cooler with the edge of a card, onto a square of paper towel, and works it back onto the new chip with his thumb. It is grey and gritty and it spreads badly. Fresh paste looks fresh. He is careful about this. It is the part that takes the thirty-five minutes.

He puts the cooler back. He runs the screws down to snug and not past it. He presses the power button.

The machine boots.

He stands there for a while waiting for something and is not sure what. The login prompt comes up at the same speed it came up yesterday, because the login prompt was never the part that needed a fast processor.

In the morning the asset agent on that machine checks in the way it does every morning, and nothing happens, and nothing keeps happening for eleven days, and on the twelfth day he pulls the regional manifest to see what it actually knows.

It knows the asset tag. It knows the hostname and the last check-in and the operating system build. It knows total physical memory as a number in gigabytes. For the processor there is a field, and in the field, on every row, are the same eight words: x64-based PC, and a family and model number that is the same across three generations of hardware. Four hundred and ten rows. Not one of them contains the word Intel.

Sixteen gigabytes is sixteen gigabytes whether it arrives as four sticks or two. The asset tag is a sticker on the front of the chassis and a row in a database, and the annual audit is a man with a tablet walking the floor, reading stickers and checking boxes, and the box he checks says the equipment was observed.

By the end of the second week he knows the check the way he knows the building.

He keeps reusing the old thermal paste for eleven more machines before he admits that nobody is going to open a case. He is a little sorry to stop. It was the part he was best at.

Machines age out. There is a form for that, a property transfer with two signature blocks, originating officer and approving supervisor. He is the approving supervisor. For the first few he finds an officer to originate, an easy conversation about a box of dead drives. Then he stops bothering and originates them himself and signs both blocks, and for the second block he uses a different pen.

The form is correct. It has the right number in the corner, both blocks filled, a date, an itemized attachment. It goes in the file. The file is a record of forms.

In March one of the sticks he put into the machine at desk four is bad. It runs fine for a month and then the machine drops in the middle of a query, and then again the next week, and the third time it happens there is somebody standing at the desk waiting for an answer. The officer opens a ticket.

A ticket is the only thing he is afraid of. A ticket brings a technician, and a technician brings a screwdriver, and a technician is the only person in the chain who has ever had a reason to look inside.

The good sticks are in a drawer at his house, in a static bag, forty dollars of memory he has not listed yet.

On Thursday he moves the machine to the desk by the printer, the one that gets used for scanning and nothing else. He changes the power plan so it never sleeps. He tells the officer he is putting her on a spare, which is true. The ticket comes back closed, unable to reproduce.

The machine by the printer stays on for the next seven months. It checks in every night at 0300. Its asset tag matches its sticker. Its memory reports sixteen gigabytes. On every report the region generates it is one of the compliant ones.

He thinks about it sometimes the way you think about a good shot. It is the cleanest thing he does the entire time.

The break room has a stack of magazines somebody's wife donated years ago and nobody has thrown out. He reads two paragraphs of one of them at 0400 while a machine posts. The Sun is missing lithium. It has less of it at the surface than it ought to, and one explanation is that early on it swallowed a planet five or ten times the size of Earth, and the way you would know is not that anything is there. The way you would know is that something is gone. He puts the magazine back face down so he can find his place, and he does not come back for it.

He is not greedy about it. Two or three machines a month, a processor here, a pair of sticks there, a drive when a drive is worth it. He never takes a whole machine. A whole machine is a hole.

In fourteen months not one of them ever says anything. They boot. They check in at 0300. They appear on the manifest with the same eight words in the processor field as every other machine in the region. He has made, by then, eight thousand two hundred dollars.

On a Sunday in October he boxes a processor for a buyer in Ohio. He wraps it in the foam it came in and tapes the seam twice, over and back, and prints the label at the kitchen table.

The printer pulls the sheet crooked and the label comes out with the barcode running off the edge. He throws it away and prints another one, and this one is straight. He peels the backing off and smooths it onto the box with his thumb, corner to corner, the way he does.

The account is in his own name. It has been since 2014, when he sold a set of golf clubs.
