Good evening. Thank you for coming out. I have brought a refrigerator.

It is behind me on a pallet. It took four people and a stair-climbing dolly and one genuinely frightening moment on the landing to get it here, and I want to say at the top that there is no reveal. Nothing is inside it. The entire demonstration is this.

I take the handle. I pull. The door opens.

That is the act. I am going to keep doing it for the next ten minutes while I talk, because it is the only thing I have to say tonight and I would rather show it to you than argue it.

Here is why it is on stage. This week a firmware update bricked a batch of Samsung smart fridges, and people stood in their kitchens and watched several hundred dollars of food go over. Not a compressor failure. Not a coolant leak. The sealed system in one of those machines is hundred-year-old technology that works so well we have stopped filing it under technology at all. What failed was the twenty-one-and-a-half-inch tablet bolted to the front. The screen where you look up recipes. And when the screen went, it took the cold with it.

Sit with the shape of that, because it is coming back about six more times tonight. The hard part worked perfectly. The easy part was load bearing.

Now. Every one of you already knows how to fix a fridge that has stopped being cold. You call somebody, they come, they charge you too much, they replace a part. That option is still available for the compressor. It is not available here, and this is the sentence I actually came to say, so I am going to say it while the door is open:

You can replace a compressor. You cannot replace a Samsung account.

That is the whole difference, and it is not a difference of difficulty. The unfixable part is never a component. A component can be sourced, bench-tested, bought used, machined by a guy in an industrial park who has been doing it since 1994. The unfixable part is always a *relationship*. A vendor. A statute. A subscription. An index. You cannot take a relationship out with a nut driver, and nobody in an industrial park has a used one on the shelf.

Watch. Open. Look in it. Nothing between me and the shelf. Still works.

Meanwhile, in Japan, the used bookshops are having their best year in decades, and it is the worst news they have had in decades, because the books are selling by the ton. Fifty tons in one order, crated and shipped to a scanning facility, and here is the part that should make your teeth hurt: to scan a book at industrial speed, you cut the spine off. You guillotine it so the pages will feed. The text comes out the far side and enters something none of us can open, and the book goes in the pulp bin. The object was fine. The object had survived seventy years in a shop in Kanda. What got severed was not the information, it was the access to it, and the two are not the same thing, which is exactly what two identical iPhones in Britain are also trying to tell us.

Because that is the other cut this week, performed with the same clean motion and no blade at all. Alice enabled Advanced Data Protection before February 2025 and her backups are end-to-end encrypted. Bill did not, and his are not, and Bill and Alice are holding the same phone, running the same OS, protected by the same mathematics, which was never weakened, never broken, never even seriously inconvenienced. What changed was a secret Technical Capability Notice and a jurisdiction flag. Same device. Different door. One of them has a handle on the inside.

And the ugliest version of it, because there is always an ugliest version, is the support number.

You want to call a company. You ask the assistant. The assistant hands you a number, warmly, with a little summary and a confident tone, and it is a scam centre in a building with a quota, because somebody flooded the web with optimised PDFs and fake support pages and the index ate them. Somebody who does brand takedowns for a living said the hard part was never finding the fake number, it was that killing one PDF just moved it to a new post by morning. And the real number, the whole time, is on the company's own website, one click, no assistant, sitting there like a fridge door. You asked the proxy. The thing was right there.

Which brings me, still holding this handle, to the tutoring company that told parents to stop paying for a human and use a model instead, and to the argument that broke out about it, which is the sharpest thing anybody said on the internet this week even though most of the people saying it did not notice what they were agreeing on. Half of them said the models are magnificent, best maths teacher I ever had, took me from trig to multivariable in a year. They are telling the truth. The other half said sure, if you already want to learn. Give it to a fifteen-year-old who would rather be on Instagram and who needed an adult in the room more than they needed an explanation, and you have not given them a tutor, you have given them an answer key with excellent manners.

Both halves are right, and the line between them is not aptitude. It is access. If you can still reach the primary thing, the proxy is a luxury and a delight and it saves you an hour. If you cannot, the proxy is all there is, and it does not care whether you learn. That is the difference between a phone with ADP and a phone without one, and it is the difference between a household that can afford a person and a household that gets told to save its money.

Last thing, and I am going to let go of the door for this, because I want both hands.

There were three good stories this week and I nearly presented them to you as three, which would have been a mistake, because they are one. An archive of Nokia industrial design, which is genuinely unpleasant to browse, wiggling dots, terrible on a phone, terrible on a desktop, and people love it. A preprint server that just got multi-year commitments to stay independent, whose entire product is an ugly page with a free PDF on it. A software store whose 2.0 announcement amounts to: there is still nothing standing between you and the binary.

Every one of them is bad at the layer everybody else optimises. No engagement surface. No account wall. No helpful intermediary. And that is not a coincidence you get to be sentimental about, it is a correlation with a mechanism: a thing with nothing in the way is a thing with nothing that can be taken away. You cannot brick a PDF. There is no firmware on a jittery archive of old phone sketches. Nobody can serve a notice on the space between you and a file, because there isn't one.

So here is the instruction, and then I will stop, and someone please help me get this back down the stairs.

Open the door and look in it. Do it with the fridge, obviously, but do it with the number on the company's own site, and the paper instead of the summary of the paper, and the kid instead of the report about the kid. It is not nostalgia and it is not a protest, it is just the shorter path, and the shorter path is the one nobody can put a toll on.

And when you find somebody still building things you can open, pay them. Not because they are noble. Because a door is an operating expense, and the reason everything else has stopped having one is that nobody was paying for the hinges.

Thank you. Good night.
