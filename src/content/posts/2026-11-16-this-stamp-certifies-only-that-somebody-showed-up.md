*To accompany the official seal of Doreen A. Pell, Notary Public, whose commission runs until the state says otherwise. Office hours Tuesday through Saturday, back room of Pell's Hardware, past the birdseed.*

People keep asking what my stamp means. Usually they ask after it has failed to mean what they hoped. So here it is, in writing, which is the only form I trust.

My stamp certifies that on a given date a person appeared before me, showed me an identification document that matched their face, and signed something. That is the whole of it. I do not read your document. I do not know if your mortgage is a good idea. I am, by law, the most modest trust signal in America, and I have come to believe that this is why I am also one of the few that works.

The Royal Society of London took as its motto *Nullius in verba*: take nobody's word for it. They put it on a coat of arms in 1663, then walked down the street to a coffeehouse and spent the next century taking each other's word for everything. I will come back to them. I have notes.

## 1. The stamp certifies the thing as written, not the thing as meant

Dr. Adaeze Okafor teaches mathematics at the community college and came in last month with a hard drive. On it, she said, was a proof of a famous theorem, checked by a computer program called Lean, and she wanted something official saying so for a grant application.

I asked to see the proof. She said it was thirteen million lines and would not open on any machine I own. The man who wrote it had needed a server with ninety-six cores and five hundred gigabytes of memory just to check it. My computer is a Dell that sometimes takes half an hour to agree to turn on. We will get to the Dell.

Dr. Okafor was very honest, which I appreciate in a customer. She explained that Lean checks that the proof proves the statement. It does not check that the statement is the one you meant. Somebody once wrote down Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean, forgot that their numbers included zero, and produced a "theorem" that zero to the third plus zero to the third equals zero to the third. True, and checked, and about as useful as my notarizing a blank page. She said that of 290 recent papers that came with Lean code, about 38 contained Lean that did not even state the paper's main result. The machine had certified each of them. It just certified something else.

"So it's like your stamp," she said.

It is exactly like my stamp, except that my stamp admits it.

Two days later Gerald Vance came in. Gerald has turned his handwriting into a font. He did this on a website that walks you through the alphabet one letter at a time and, to its credit, never uploads anything. Now Gerald can type a letter to his sister that looks handwritten, and he wanted me to certify that the signature at the bottom, which he had typed, was his.

I notarized Gerald. I did not notarize the font. Gerald's signature checks out the same way Dr. Okafor's theorem does: the form is flawless. Whether Gerald meant it, or was even in the room when it was typed, is someone else's problem. My stamp was invented to solve that problem, and it does so the old-fashioned way: by requiring Gerald, in person, with his license, smelling faintly of the birdseed aisle.

## 2. The stamp certifies the moment, not the future

Every impression of my seal carries a date. People think the date is paperwork. It is the most honest thing on the page. It says: this was true on Tuesday. After Tuesday, you are on your own.

Lyle from the Lions Club came in white as a sheet. Fifteen years ago he printed a fundraising flyer with a short link on it, the kind with a string of letters after the slash. The link company let the old link lapse, and someone else registered the same letters and pointed them somewhere vile. Lyle's flyer, still taped in the Methodist church basement, now sends anyone with a phone camera to a place Lyle cannot repeat in front of his wife.

He is in excellent company. On Sunday morning somebody re-created the short link from a 2011 Burger King tweet that once pointed to a restaurant locator, and aimed it at Wikipedia's list of Nazi concentration camps. Eighteen minutes earlier, a 2011 McDonald's link had been aimed at the September 11 article. Nobody hacked Burger King. Nobody needed to. The link company's help pages promised these letters could never be reused. That promise was a stamp with no date on it, which is to say a lie that had not been tested yet.

Here is the trick, Lyle: put a plus sign on the end of the link and it will show you the date it was created. If that date is newer than your flyer, the link is not your link anymore. A plus sign. The whole internet's provenance problem, and the fix is the same as my seal: tell people when.

Compare the Internet Archive, which has kept about 250,000 articles from Salon, a magazine that just announced it is ceasing publication after thirty-one years. The Archive does not vouch that any of those articles was right. Plenty of them, I'd guess, nobody would sign today. It vouches only that on such-and-such a date, at such-and-such an address, these were the words. That is a notary's journal with a very large back room. It is trustworthy precisely because it refuses to update.

## 3. The stamp certifies a relation, not a fact

Bernadette Ruiz bought rain boots on Black Friday for $126.99. The listing said DEAL in red. Nine days earlier the same boots were $79.97. She came in to ask whether a red DEAL label was a legally binding statement, and if so whether I could notarize her outrage.

I could not. But a research group went through more than 350 of those handpicked holiday deals and found that nearly one in three were no cheaper than the month before, and nearly one in ten cost more. One pair of men's boots was 83 percent more expensive on Cyber Monday than four weeks earlier. The label was not exactly false. It was a relation. It meant "less than some number," and nobody would say which number, or when.

While Bernadette was at the counter, she showed me on her phone that the eighth most popular website in the world is a thing called w2rt6byl5nf1opmc-a.com. It ranks above Microsoft. It was registered around the eleventh of this month. It does not load. One company counts about 241 million links pointing to it from five million sites. Another company, looking at the same internet, counts 53.

"Which one is right?" Bernadette asked.

Neither, I told her. Both. A ranking is a crowd of witnesses, and a crowd can be rented. The Royal Society knew this, by the way. When Mr. Boyle ran his air pump, he invited gentlemen to watch, because a fact witnessed by enough respectable people became a fact. They took nobody's word for it, except the words of several nobodies in good coats. That works right up until the coats are sold by the million.

Bernadette returned the boots. She asked me to stamp the receipt anyway. I did, and it certified the one thing in this story that was beyond dispute: Bernadette, on a Tuesday, furious.

## 4. The stamp is not given to whoever asks nicely

I ask for identification. I cannot stress enough that this is the entire job. Not reading, not judging. Just: who are you, and can you prove it?

My nephew uses a program called uBlock Origin to block ads, which he says is a security measure and not a convenience, and I believe him, having seen the ads. It turns out that for a while, part of that program would take instructions from any web page that sent them. A hostile page could quietly open the program's own picker tool, fill in a rule of its choosing, and wait for you to click something harmless-looking. One click and the page had written itself a permission slip for every other website you visit. The fix was a secret token, so the tool now checks who is talking to it before it obeys. Congratulations. It has invented asking for ID.

Now for the Dell. I do not tell this part proudly.

When the Dell would not turn on, I called a help line, and a man I have never met, whose name I did not verify, told me to unplug it, take out the battery, press the power button ten times at one-second intervals, hold it down for thirty seconds, put the battery back in, and try again. He said it was "static discharge." He could not explain further. There is a 2007 blog post from a fellow whose ThinkPad died before a conference, and the IBM support line gave him the same ritual, and for years afterward strangers left comments under it saying thank you, it worked, it didn't, it worked the second time.

I did it. I counted out loud. It worked. I would like the record to show that a woman who checks two forms of ID before stamping a lease got down on the floor of her own hardware store and performed a rain dance on the instructions of a disembodied voice, because I needed the computer and there was no one else to ask.

Here is where Dr. Okafor came back. She had found the answer to her Lean problem. There is a tool that checks that the theorem proved is the theorem you wrote down, uses only the standard rules, and in its paranoid mode has the proof checked again by several independently built checkers. All of them have to agree.

"So," I said, "three notaries who don't know each other."

She thought about it and said yes, more or less.

## 5. The stamp does not mean I believe you

This is the clause people like least.

I don't believe you, and I don't disbelieve you. Belief is above my pay grade, which is set by the state at a figure I will not embarrass myself by printing. The fellow who found that uBlock bug wrote at the bottom that a machine helped him find it and he wrote all the prose himself. Another fellow who wrote about the mystery domain put a "100% Human" badge on his site, then admitted the badge was out of sync because he'd used up his credits. I trust both men more for saying what their badges don't cover. That is the whole trick. It is the only trick.

Which brings me back to the Royal Society, who I promised would get their due.

*Nullius in verba* was never possible. Not in 1663 and not now. Nobody can check everything. Dr. Okafor cannot read thirteen million lines. I cannot rebuild the link company. Mr. Boyle's gentlemen could not all build their own air pumps. What actually built modern science was not taking nobody's word. It was a room where you could see the face of the man whose word you were taking, where you'd see him again next Thursday, where somebody else at the table would say "that's not what you said last week," and where the coffee was strong enough to keep everyone honest until closing. That is not distrust. It is trust with a return address.

My stamp is a very small coffeehouse. It has a name, a face, a date, and a commission number. It tells you exactly how little it means. That is why it means anything at all.

## 6. Exception

There is one stamp I issue without identification.

On Saturdays, Bernadette's grandson Mateo comes in with her. He is six. He has no license, no passport, and no fixed opinion about rain boots. He stands at the counter until I take out the other stamp, the rubber one shaped like a duck, and press it on the back of his hand.

It is not my official seal. The state would have words. But it is the only stamp I own that means precisely what it says, no more and no less, with nothing in the fine print.

It certifies that Mateo was here.

*Doreen A. Pell, Notary Public. This document has been signed in person, in ink, in the presence of the birdseed.*
