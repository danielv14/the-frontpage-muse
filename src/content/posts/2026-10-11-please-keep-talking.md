The call was at half past two on a Thursday, and Deb Stanczyk had the laptop propped on a bread board so the camera would not be looking directly up into her chin. This was the only trick she had learned from doing four of these. Nobody warns you that a webcam takes an interest in your jaw.

"Can you hear me? Sorry. I've got a list," said the man. His name was Tom. He was somewhere with a bicycle hanging on the wall behind him. "I'm supposed to read you the list."

"Read me the list."

"Right. So: share your screen, go at whatever pace feels natural, and say everything out loud. Everything, even the stupid bits. Take your time, there's no wrong answer."

"Okay."

"That's the list."

Twenty-five euros for the hour, which would land in her account as twenty-one pounds and some change, because somewhere between his bank and hers a machine took a bite. She shared her screen. The project was called ActivityBot and she had never heard of it, which was the point, and she opened the repository and began to read out loud like a woman reading a menu in a language she had three words of.

"I'm scrolling," she said. "Still scrolling. There is a lot of README."

"Mm," said Tom, and typed.

Step two wanted her to put the tool on her path and gave her a line to paste, and inside the double quotes of that line sat a tilde. She read it the way you read a word you have known since you were nine. It would work on his machine. It would work on nearly everybody's machine. It would not work on hers, because her home was not where his assumed it was and the quotes meant the shell would hand that little squiggle along untouched, a hair in the soup of the path, and the error it produced forty minutes later would say nothing whatsoever about tildes. She typed the real line instead, said "okay, path, done," and moved on. The whole thing took about four seconds. On the other end, Tom typed nothing at all, because nothing had happened.

Twenty-two years of keeping other people's systems breathing, a good stretch of them for people who were never going to pay her, and this is what it buys you: you can hear a thing coming the way you hear a dishwasher about to finish.

What she had not expected, the first time she did one of these, was how easy the document was to read in the other direction.

Section three had been hurt recently. You could see it. The instruction about renaming the config file had a parenthesis on the end of it that was twice the length of the instruction itself, explaining that this includes the hidden ones, which your file manager will not show you unless you ask it to, and here is how you ask it. Nobody writes that sentence first. Somebody writes that sentence at nine o'clock at night after watching a stranger on a video call hunt for a file that was sitting right there the whole time, invisible, three inches from the cursor. The politeness of it was the giveaway. It was the most careful sentence on the page and it was carefully only about one tiny thing.

Section four had a different injury. There was a line in it that landed with a small shrug on the end, a half beat of rhythm with nothing standing in it, and she knew that shape too. A joke had been there. Someone had told him it was not funny, or worse, had asked him sincerely what it meant, and he had taken the joke out and left the shrug behind like the nail hole after a picture comes down.

"I'm reading this bit again," she said.

"That's useful, actually. Why?"

"Because I've read it twice."

Bishop arrived on the table at 2:51, as he did, grey and enormous and entirely without a plan, and stood on the F keys for a while with the specific weight of a cat who has been told before. She moved him once. He came back. She let him stay.

She got to the part about the web server and stopped, and for a moment she let herself wonder, as she had wondered somewhere around step two, whether a person could make a door this stiff on purpose. There was a whole reading of the document in which the hard parts were the point, in which the stiffness was a sieve, and the people who made it through to the other side were exactly the people he wanted, and everyone else was supposed to give up in the hallway and go home. She had met people who built like that. She had been, for about eighteen months in her thirties, a person who built like that. She genuinely could not tell from here which one Tom was, and she suspected that from in there, with his bicycle and his notes and his apologetic list, he could not tell either.

"Sorry," said Tom. "You went quiet."

"I'm thinking about permissions."

"Mm," said Tom, who had no idea, and typed.

Section six was smooth.

She noticed it the way you notice a road surface change under a bike. No parentheses. No second sentence doing damage control on the first. Nothing numbered 3b, wedged in sideways, the way things get numbered when they are added after the numbers. It went straight down the page in the clean, unhedged, pleased-with-itself prose of a man explaining something he understands perfectly to someone he has never met, and every single person before her had got here with forty minutes gone and their good will spent and had nodded along and said yes, fine, that makes sense, and had not stopped.

So she stopped.

She let the silence go on long enough to be a real silence. She read the first line of it aloud, and then read it aloud again, slower, which is a thing people do when they are stuck and never when they are performing. She moved the pointer up to the sentence and left it sitting there, hovering, so that it would be on the recording. She said, "hang on." She opened a terminal and did not type anything in it, which she had seen a genuinely confused person do on her own screen share years ago and had never forgotten, the way the hands go somewhere familiar while the head is lost.

"Deb? Sorry. Please keep talking."

"I'm here," she said. "I'm just not sure what this is asking me to do."

She said it the way she would have said it at twenty-four. She put the confusion down precisely where it needed to be, like setting a glass on a coaster, and then she waited, and she let it be awkward, and she did not help.

On the other end of the call the typing started, and went on, and went on, much longer than it had gone on all afternoon. Bishop got his front feet up onto the bread board. The tea had been cold for half an hour. It was ten past three, and outside the kitchen window somebody three doors down was failing to start a car.
