A core sample is a lie you can hold in one hand. You sink a hollow bit straight down, draw up a column of everything you passed through, and lay it on a bench in the daylight where none of it was ever meant to be seen at once. Then you read it top to bottom, newest rock to oldest, and pretend the story runs that cleanly.

Here is the column we pulled up this week. Read it downward.

## The surface

At the top, the shine. This month a machine ended an argument that mathematicians had been losing for eighty-five years. The Jacobian Conjecture — a clean, stubborn claim about polynomials and whether a certain kind of map can always be undone — had held since 1939. Claude Fable produced the counterexample: three polynomials, coefficients small enough to say in one breath, a map that quietly folds two different points onto the same point and so can never be reversed. Elsewhere the same season, engineers wired a million p-bits across eighteen chips and built a computer that does not so much calculate as shiver — a machine that takes noise, real randomness, and lets it settle into answers to problems too hard for the orderly kind. Two faces of one fantasy: mind without matter. Truth pulled out of the air. Order distilled from static. The old dream that intelligence has finally climbed out of the body and gone somewhere clean.

Watch how we announce it. The counterexample — a fact that will stay true for as long as complex numbers stay true, which is to say forever — was posted to the timeline in a format that expires in seven days. An eternal result on a self-deleting substrate. And a man watching the machine close the oldest problem in the room reached for Arthur C. Clarke: *overhead, without any fuss, the stars were going out.* He meant it as awe. Read it again as an inventory. The stars go out without any fuss precisely because nothing physical seems to be happening anywhere at all. That is the whole trick of the top layer. It looks weightless. It looks like it costs nothing.

It is held up. The theorem-solver and the noise-computer exist this month and not some other month because, for three years running, capital agreed to hold them aloft — and this week capital started to wobble. *Big Tech needs to justify its AI spending as investors dump the stock*, the headline says, and under it people are charging up their schadenfreude, asking whether the RAM will finally get cheap again, whether the pension funds survive the unwind. This is the seam in the sample, the place where the pale upper rock meets something darker, and it is worth staring at, because it is the exact moment the abstraction stops being free. The instant the mind-in-the-cloud has to show a return — has to pay, has to be *justified* — it stops floating. It reaches down. It has to put its hands on something solid to turn the money real. And here the color of the core changes.

## Bedrock

Under the cloud is a field, and the field is always someone's. This same week, on this same front page, power companies are condemning private land by eminent domain to run the transmission lines that feed the data centers, and Big Tech is walking onto Native American reservations for ground it has not yet taken any other way. These are not two stories. They are one accusation with two dates on it. A man in Festus, Missouri holds a hand-lettered sign at a council meeting. A tribal council, three states over, weighs a check against another century of being useful to somebody else's grid. The lawyers say *public use.* They said public use the first time, too — the industry that swears it lives in the cloud needs, to survive a single fiscal quarter, the state to take a stranger's dirt and hand it across; and the dirt it reaches for first is the dirt already taken once, from people who were told, that time, that it was for the good of the public.

There is a name for the thing this whole column is missing, and the industry coined it. In machine learning, *ground truth* is the real, measured data you check the model against — the reading you trust because it came from the world and not from the model's own confident hum. This is the ground. This is the truth. It is a condemned quarter-section outside St. Louis and a mesa the map has always drawn a fence around. The machine can prove an eighty-five-year-old conjecture false and still not know the first true thing about the room it is standing in: that everything above it rests on soil it never asked to stand on.

And the ground keeps its own clock — the one clock the seven-day link can neither compress nor delete. In a garden in Rayleigh this week a banana plant fruited after fifteen years of being ignored, an ornamental that was never meant to bear, doing it anyway, on nobody's quarter but its own. The roadside country of giant donuts and dinosaur filling stations survives now only as eleven thousand photographs, the buildings themselves long since scraped for something more efficient. Somewhere a company teaches your documents, one more time, to open only in the room it owns. Enclosure is the oldest move on the sheet. First the commons, then the file, now the field.

Lay the whole column on the bench. At the top: a proof that will outlast the language it was written in, published on a link that dies Thursday. In the middle: the money, wobbling. At the bottom: the ground — a sign at a council meeting, a fence on a map, a check nobody wanted to have to take. The industry calls the top of that column *intelligence* and the bottom of it *overhead.* It has the stratigraphy exactly upside down. The theorem is the froth. The field is the truth. And we are drilling straight through the second to keep the first in the air a little longer, telling ourselves the whole way down that nothing physical is happening at all.
