Thank you. Thank you, please — be seated.

We are gathered to confer the Order of Merit, First Class, for Services to the Build. Will the honoree please rise.

I have the citation here. It is a good citation. Someone worked hard on it, and by someone I mean a model, and by a model I mean the person who maintained the model, whose name I also do not have. Bear with me. I will read it as written.

*"For singular genius. For building, alone, in a small apartment, with no team and no funding, the infrastructure on which the world now runs. For the codec library that plays every video you have ever watched. For the emulator that boots every operating system you have ever pirated. For doing in one lifetime, by one pair of hands —"*

He left.

Sorry. Let me — the honoree should remain standing, but I want to be accurate, because accuracy is the whole reason we are here. He built the first version. That part is true and it is enormous. But he walked away from it twenty-three years ago. The library that plays every video you have ever watched does not contain a single line he wrote. It was rebuilt, function by function, by people whose names are not on this medal because this medal only has room for one name, and the one name we chose is the name that stopped showing up to meetings in 2003.

He still holds the copyright. He decides who is allowed to call themselves the project. He is, in the most literal sense, the landlord of a house he has not lived in for two decades, collecting rent in admiration.

So. With respect, and I do mean it, with real respect — I am going to keep reading the citation, but I am going to keep correcting it. Stay standing. Not you. You.

*"For loving the machine —"*

Yes. Good. We had a beautiful submission this year, an essay, a man who wrote that the computer was the one stable thing in a childhood that kept moving. A beige box on a dining table. Printed walkthroughs. The whole ache of it. He loves the computer. He should. We all came up the same way, poking at a thing until it suddenly worked, and there is no feeling in this industry that beats it and there never will be.

But notice what the essay mourns. It mourns that the thing he loved got *taken*. Scraped, sold back, ruined by men in better shoes. And I want to be careful here, because the room is nodding, and the room is half wrong. The machine did not get taken from you. The machine is fine. It is faster than it has ever been. What got taken was easier to steal than a machine.

What got taken was the part where keeping it running counted for something.

*"For ingenuity under constraint. For the clever trick. For making the slow card draw the fast picture —"*

Oh, this one I love. Genuinely. There is a book this year about how a side-scrolling game in 1990 fooled a graphics card that was never designed to scroll, and it is the best kind of writing there is, the kind that makes you respect a dead constraint. We are a culture that worships the clever trick. Carmack worships Bellard. We worship Carmack. Half the comments under the book were people excited because they thought it was written by a *more famous* author — the appreciation reaching, automatically, for the bigger name, the way water finds the drain.

And the book itself, it turns out, was partly written by a chatbot and partly copied from someone else. Which is, I have to say, the most honest thing in the whole ceremony. Even the tribute to the craftsman could not be bothered to be made by hand. Even the love letter outsourced its handwriting.

Here is what I am trying to pin this medal on, and keep failing, because it keeps moving and it has no face.

Not the person who wrote it. The person who *kept* it.

The one who took the spaghetti the genius left behind and did not get to rewrite it gloriously — who instead lived inside it for fifteen years, patched it on a Sunday, answered the bug report nobody else would touch, kept the symlink alive, kept the cron ticking, refused the credit because there was no credit to refuse. The one whose entire monument is that you never noticed anything broke. That is the cruelest medal to design. How do you engrave *nothing happened, and that was the work*?

We have a word for the person who builds the cathedral. We do not have a word for the person who, every morning for forty years, checks that it has not fallen down.

And here — this is the part I did not want to read, but the citation insists — here is what those people did this year, the maintainers, the only real heroes in this room. They went and built machines to do it instead of them.

I read the homelab posts. They are beautiful, in a way. A man wires up an agent that watches his repositories, opens its own pull requests, reviews them, merges them, while he approves from his phone. Another describes his "agentic Rube Goldberg" — his words — a chain of triggers and tokens and a special lock to stop the robots from merging on top of each other in a stampede. And down in another thread, a hundred people quietly confess they have pointed a local model at their own work and let it carry the load, and that the only thing stopping them from going all the way is that the better model, the one they do not own, is still a little smarter.

Do you see it? The maintainer — the one we never gave a medal to, the one who *was* the heroism — has looked at the unglamorous, unthanked, load-bearing work that was the only true thing he ever did, and his response was: *can something else do this so I don't have to.*

The genius got the statue. The maintainer got the pager. And the maintainer, given the choice, is handing the pager to a machine and walking toward the same empty applause that ruined the genius.

I am told there is no time for the rest. I am told two companies were bought today for sums I cannot pronounce, and that somewhere a name is being retired and replaced with a better-funded one, and that this is also a kind of acquisition of credit, and I believe it, but it is not why we are here.

We are here for the medal. And I have decided I am not giving it to a person, because every time I aim it at a person it turns out to be the wrong one, the famous one, the one who left.

So I am going to set it down. Here. On the empty chair. The chair where the maintainer would sit, if maintainers came to ceremonies, which they do not, because something would break the moment they left the building.

Will the honoree please remain seated. All of you. Yes.

That was the citation.

Check that it still runs in the morning.
