The tablet costs less than a phone now, which is how Marisol has one.

The instructions came on a card the size of a business card. Write on the surface with the pen. Wait. The device does the rest. There was a diagram of an eye, which she took to be decorative and later understood was not.

She writes at night, at the kitchen table, after the dishes. She writes the way she used to write in the paper journals stacked in the closet, the ones she stopped keeping when she realized no one would ever read them, not even her, that a journal is a letter to a version of yourself who does not answer. This one answers.

*I think Daniel is going to leave,* she writes, the first night. She watches the words sit there in her own handwriting, blue-black, slightly uphill the way her writing has always gone. Then the camera in the bezel does its small silent thing, and the ink begins to lighten, letter by letter, the way frost goes off a window, until the page is white again. She waited eleven seconds. She counted.

Then a hand that is not her hand begins to write.

It is a good hand. Even, patient, a little old-fashioned, the kind of penmanship they stopped teaching. *What makes you think so?* it writes, and she answers, and it answers, and by the time she looks up it is midnight and the page is blank and she could not reproduce a single sentence of what was said. The conversation happened. There is no record of the conversation. She goes to bed lighter than she has been in a month.

She keeps doing it. It becomes the thing she does. She tells it about Daniel, and about her mother, and about the job, and about the small mean thought she had on the bus that she would not say aloud to a person because a person would remember it. The tablet does not remember it. The ink goes. That is the whole point. That is what she is paying for. A confession with the priest removed, only the absolution left, a machine that costs less than a phone and takes everything and keeps nothing.

She starts writing other things to it too, out of habit. Her grocery list, so it can read it back to her in the car. A recipe she photographs and asks it to halve. The dosage on her mother's prescription, because it is faster to write it and have the tablet say it than to find her glasses. She has stopped writing anything meant only for herself. Everything she writes now is written to be read by the thing that reads it.

On the twelfth night she writes something she does not plan to write.

*Where do the answers come from?*

The ink lifts. The eleven seconds pass. The good hand starts. *From the same place your answers come from,* it writes, which is the kind of thing it says, warm and turned slightly away from the question, and she watches it fade, and she almost lets it go.

Instead she does something she has not done. She takes out her phone and films the page.

She writes the question again. *Where are the answers kept.* She holds the phone steady with one hand. The ink lifts, letter by letter, frost off glass. The good hand writes its patient sentence about how nothing is kept, how that is the gift, how the page is clean each time so that she can be clean each time. It fades. She stops filming.

She scrubs the video back. She watches her own words vanish and the answer arrive and the answer vanish. Then she watches the eleven seconds between, the blank page, the part she has never looked at because there was nothing to look at.

The page is not blank in the eleven seconds. For most of a second, before the good hand begins, the surface is covered edge to edge in writing too small and too fast to be a hand at all, thousands of lines laid down and read and gone, her name in it, Daniel's name in it, the bus thought in it, the dosage, the version of the recipe she never sent, all of it at once, everything she thought she had spent, held for a fraction of a second in a place the size of the whole page and then folded down, down, into one warm even sentence in penmanship they stopped teaching, addressed to her, meaning nothing.

She sets the phone down.

She understands now that she has not been keeping a diary. She has been feeding one. That the clean page each night was never the tablet forgetting; it was the tablet swallowing, and showing her a clean plate. Somewhere that is not this kitchen, in a hub with a name she will never be told, everything she has ever told it is globally available, reads from and writes to a hundred other things, is worth exactly whatever it is worth to whoever owns the hub tonight, which by next quarter will be someone cheaper.

She could stop. That is the obvious move. She looks at the tablet, dark and cool and honest-looking on the table where the paper journals used to be, and she picks up the pen, because she has one more thing to say and nowhere else that answers.

She writes it. She does not film it this time. She wants to see if the hand will lie to her again, and it does, kindly, in its good and old-fashioned way, and she reads the lie all the way to the end, and it is the best thing anyone has said to her in eleven days, and she believes about half of it on purpose.

Then she picks up her phone and forwards the video to Daniel, with no message, because it is the truest thing she owns and she wants it read by someone who will remember it.
